Can Auditors Predict the Choices Made by Other Auditors?
Abstract
An implicit assumption of prior literature on strategic behavior of auditors is that auditors know
the preferences of their colleagues. In this study, we conduct an experiment to investigate the
validity of this assumption. In our experiment, we match a manager with a pair of top and
mediocre audit seniors, as assessed by their firm. Each auditor predicts the choices that will be
made by other auditors on two tasks that differ in their level of ambiguity. Our results show no
difference in the accuracy among managers, top seniors, and mediocre seniors when they predict
the choices made by specific individual auditors for both tasks. When predicting the number of
managers and seniors who will choose a specific option on the high-ambiguity task, managers
outperform top seniors, who in turn outperform mediocre seniors. For the low-ambiguity task,
we find no difference among managers, top seniors, and mediocre seniors. Our results provide
some limited support for models of strategic auditor behavior, and indicate that the ability to
predict the choices of others is a dimension of an auditor’s expertise.

Can Auditors Predict the Choices Made by Other Auditors?
1. Introduction
Existing literature suggests that auditors adapt their behavior to conform to the
preferences of their superiors (e.g., Gibbins and Newton [1994]; Peecher [1996]), and that
superiors anticipate this behavior and attempt to undo it (Rich, Solomon, and Trotman
[1997]). An implicit assumption underlying these studies is that auditors know the
preferences of other auditors. However, evidence on the validity of this assumption is
lacking. We provide some evidence on this issue in this paper.
Conceptually, preferences are the values (utility) assigned by decision makers to
specific options/actions (Varian [1992]), and are generally operationalized by specific
methods of elicitation such as examining the choices made by decision makers among
options (e.g., Berg et al. [1986]; Tversky and Simonson [1993]). In this study, we infer
auditors’ preferences from the choices they make, and investigate their ability to predict
the choices of their peers, superiors, and subordinates. In our experiment, we pair each
audit manager with two seniors with whom he/she normally works. The pair of audit
seniors has been assessed by the firm as being either outstanding or mediocre (we call
them ‘top’ and ‘mediocre’ seniors). We design the tasks so that they vary in the extent to
which there is ambiguity regarding the choices that will be made by other auditors. Our
results show no difference in the accuracy among managers, top seniors, and mediocre
seniors when they predict the choices that will be made by specific individual auditors
for both tasks. However, these tests lack power due to the small sample sizes. For the
high-ambiguity task, however, managers outperform top seniors, who in turn outperform
mediocre seniors when they predict the aggregate number of managers and seniors
1

choosing particular options. For the low-ambiguity task, there are no performance
differences among the managers, top seniors, and mediocre seniors.
Our study focuses on audit tasks for which there are no clear objectively
determined correct answers (e.g., assessing whether the additional risk from selling to a
new class of customers should be reflected in the form of an additional allowance for
doubtful debts or a footnote disclosure).1 For such tasks, the choices made by other
auditors in the firm serve as an important benchmark for assessing performance (Emby
and Gibbins [1988]); accordingly, knowledge of what choices others will make should aid
the performance of these tasks.
Our results documenting whether auditors can accurately predict the choices of
their superiors has implications on whether auditors can successfully stylize workpapers
to influence their reviewers, as suggested by the literature on justification (e.g., Gibbins
and Newton [1994]; Peecher [1996]; Rich, Solomon, and Trotman [1997]). In turn,
determining whether auditors can predict accurately the choices of their subordinates has
implications on whether reviewers are able to recognize and/or overcome attempts by
subordinates to stylize their workpapers (Rich, Solomon, and Trotman [1997]). Our
study also extends work on tacit managerial knowledge (e.g., Tan and Libby [1997]) and
justification (Gibbins and Newton [1994]; Peecher [1996]) in that we show that the
ability to predict the choices of other auditors is associated with audit expertise.
Specifically, our results suggest that managers are relatively better strategic monitors of
the environment than are top seniors, who in turn are better monitors than mediocre
seniors in that they can more accurately predict the choices made by other managers and
other seniors.

2. Background and Hypothesis Development
The psychology and experimental economics literature generally characterizes
people as biased in estimating the choices made by specific individuals (Camerer [1990],
Fussell and Kraus [1992], Ross, Green and House [1977]) and groups (Judd and Johnson
[1981]). The key reason for this poor performance appears to be an “availability” effect
(Tversky and Kahneman [1973]) ¾ a person’s own preferences are particularly salient,
and there is a tendency for people to overestimate the commonality of their own
preferences when predicting the choices made by others (Fussell and Kraus [1992]).
People appear to anchor on their own preferences and insufficiently adjust for differences
in others’ preferences (Camerer [1990]; Tversky and Kahneman [1973]). Using multiple
choice audit/accounting questions, Kennedy and Peecher [1997] show that auditors are
overconfident in judging the technical knowledge of their subordinates, and rely on
perceptions of their own knowledge in judging others’ knowledge.
Although the task of predicting the choices of others is difficult, certain features
of the audit environment may lead to improved performance. Some tasks contain
characteristics that enable people to achieve success in coordinating (and predicting)
other people’s behavior (Schelling [1960]). These tasks have psychological “focal
points,” which are naturally occurring contextual features that signal how others are
likely to behave (Schelling [1960]). In an auditing context, some audit tasks have no
clear objectively determined correct answer, and the preferences of the auditors making
the decision play a key role in determining their responses. Some of these tasks contain
characteristics that indicate the likely choices taken by auditors; other tasks do not. For
example, to assess the existence and validity of accounts receivable, various procedures
3

can be employed (confirmation, verification of shipping documents, or verification of
subsequent cash receipts). Confirmation of accounts receivable is one of the most
effective and efficient methods to achieve this objective (Arens and Loebbecke [1997],
and awareness of this feature provides a cue on the likely choice of procedure adopted by
other auditors. In contrast, where the task is to ascertain the valuation of accounts
receivable for a new product line being sold to a more risky class of customers,
professional standards provide substantial flexibility on the appropriate action that can be
taken (i.e., either footnote disclosure or an additional allowance for doubtful debts). In
this case, there are fewer cues on the choice likely to be adopted by the auditor, and the
task of predicting the choices of other auditors becomes more difficult.
The knowledge of the auditor may facilitate the prediction of the choices made
by other auditors. Extant literature has shown that tacit managerial knowledge
¾ knowledge about managing self, managing others, and managing career (Wagner and
Sternberg [1985]; Tan and Libby [1997]) ¾ is associated with superior performance
evaluation in the audit firm. One component of tacit managerial knowledge —
knowledge about managing others ¾ is particularly germane to our discussion. This
knowledge relates to knowledge about how to manage one’s subordinates, superiors, and
peers, and includes knowledge about the preferences of these colleagues. To develop
this knowledge about the other auditors’ preferences, we suggest that two aspects are
critical: interactions with other auditors, and auditors’ sensitivity to other auditors’
preferences.
Ceteris paribus, auditors who have more interactions with their colleagues
should have better knowledge of their colleagues’ preferences. This argument implies
4

that audit managers, who generally have more experience both in their job level and in
total years of audit experience, should have more opportunities than audit seniors to
interact with other auditors and learn their preferences. The frequency of interactions per
se may not be a sufficient condition for auditors to acquire the necessary knowledge of
other auditors’ preferences. For example, although two audit seniors (a top senior and a
mediocre senior) may have the same amount of interaction with other auditors, the
auditor with superior overall performance is likely to be more sensitive to the preferences
of other auditors and/or have the ability to acquire such knowledge (Tan and Libby
[1997]). This suggests that relative to mediocre seniors, top seniors and managers (who
are generally promoted from the pool of top seniors) should have better knowledge of the
preferences of other auditors with whom they work, and are therefore better able to
predict their choices.
It is unlikely that managers and top seniors will be uniformly better than
mediocre seniors in predicting the choices made by others across tasks differing in
ambiguity. In particular, as discussed earlier, some tasks contain cues that facilitate the
prediction of choices made by others; hence, managers and top seniors should have less
performance advantage for such tasks (Tan and Kao [1999]). In contrast, for tasks with
few such cues, predicting the choices made by other auditors becomes difficult unless
one has previously learned their preferences.
In terms of predicting other auditors’ choices, an auditor can focus on specific
individual auditors or a group of auditors in their firm. An auditor may need to know the
choices made by a specific auditor (e.g., his/her immediate supervisor) because this may
enable him/her to perform work that will be more acceptable to the supervisor (Tan and
5

Libby [1997]; Rich, Solomon, and Trotman [1997]). At the same time, knowledge of
what other auditors in the firm would do is also useful because this enables an
assessment of the likely distribution of choices (opinions) on an issue within the firm. In
the absence of a clear objectively determined right/wrong answer, the choices made by
others provide a useful benchmark with which to assess decisions. For example, Emby
and Gibbins’ [1988, p. 294] study shows that auditors believe that a judgment is good if
“another person agrees with you, or the decision was similar to those others have
chosen.”
Statistics theory suggests that it should be easier to predict the aggregate choices
of several individuals (i.e., the number of individuals choosing a particular option) than
that of a specific individual (i.e., the option chosen by one particular individual) because
error variances are smaller for group means than individual means. Related arguments
and evidence by Ravinder, Kleinmuntz and Dyer [1988] and Kleinmuntz, Fennema and
Peecher [1996] also show that, sometimes, more accurate probability assessments can be
made by aggregating probability assessments of individual components rather than by
making holistic probability assessments. However, there is a large psychology literature
showing that intuitive human judgment sometimes departs from normative rules (e.g.,
Kahneman and Tversky [1973]). This literature reports mixed results on whether there is
any difference in the relative accuracy of assessing the choices of a specific individual as
opposed to the frequencies of group members making a particular choice. For example,
cognitive biases are sometimes reduced when individuals make assessments of aggregate
frequencies as opposed to that of single events (e.g., Gigerenzer [1991]; Gigerenzer and
Hoffrage [1995]) but are sometimes not (e.g., Brenner et al. [1996]; Kahneman and
6

Tversky [1996]). Because there are no strong a priori reasons to anticipate whether
predictions of individual versus aggregate choices will be more accurate, we do not offer
any directional hypotheses on individual versus aggregate choices. Instead, we assume
that our hypotheses will apply to both types of predictions.
In the next few sub-sections, we describe two tasks (a bad-debt task and a
control-weakness task) and develop hypotheses on how different auditor groups
(managers, top seniors, and mediocre seniors) can predict the choices made by other
auditors on these tasks.
2.1 HIGH-AMBIGUITY TASK
We first consider a bad-debt task (adapted from Hackenbrack and Nelson
[1996]) where auditors must decide whether to accede to a long-standing client’s request
to provide a footnote disclosure about the adequacy of its bad debt provision, or to insist
on an additional allowance for bad debts. Some of the client’s accounts receivable are
overdue, but they relate to a new product for which the debt collectability history is
unknown. The quantum of the allowance needed is not material relative to the client’s
assets, but because the client’s profits are low, the additional allowance may lead the
client to violate its debt covenants.
We consider this bad-debt task highly ambiguous. For this task, there are
compelling factors in favor of either footnote disclosure or an additional provision. The
task does not contain features that signal the likely action that most auditors would take.
To predict the choices made by others for this task, auditors need to be aware of at least
two aspects. First, auditors must be aware that there are arguments in favor of either
decision (footnote disclosure or additional provision), and there is, therefore, no clear
7

correct answer. Second, they must know the preferences of other auditors to assess
which set of factors these other auditors are more likely to favor. In particular, the
extent to which these other managers and seniors are conservative when reviewing
client’s accounting estimates would be relevant information to make accurate
predictions.
For this high-ambiguity task, knowledge of the preferences of other auditors is
an important component towards its successful completion (Emby and Gibbins [1988]).
We anticipate that, relative to mediocre seniors, managers and top seniors should be
more cognizant of the importance of the preferences of others as an input to successful
performance (e.g., Tan and Libby [1997]), and will be more sensitive to the preferences
of other auditors. Hence, we expect that managers and top seniors will be better able
than mediocre seniors to predict the choices of other managers and other seniors.
Compared to top seniors, managers should also have more opportunities over time to
learn the preferences of other auditors (seniors and managers); hence, we also expect
managers to be able to predict the choices made by other auditors better than top seniors.
Our hypotheses are summarized below:
H1: For the high-ambiguity task, managers and top seniors will outperform mediocre
seniors in terms of their ability to predict the choices made by other auditors.
H2: For the high-ambiguity task, managers will outperform top seniors in terms of
their ability to predict choices made by other auditors.
2.2 LOW-AMBIGUITY TASK
While Hypotheses 1 and 2 imply that job level (manager, top senior, mediocre
senior) has a direct influence on auditors’ predictive accuracy in the high-ambiguity task,
8

the nature of the task can interact with job level to influence auditors’ predictive
accuracy. In this sub-section, we describe how the effect of job level on predictive
accuracy (described in Section 2.3) may be moderated in the case of a low-ambiguity
task.
Consider a control-weakness task (adapted from Arens and Loebbecke [1997]),
where two internal control weaknesses (pertaining to premature recording of sales and
improper credit authorization) have been detected during the interim audit. For this task,
time and budget constraints necessitate that a decision be made on which of the two
issues is more important and requires immediate follow up during the interim audit. This
task has no clear correct answer, particularly since both weaknesses should be
investigated at some point during the audit. However, relative to the bad-debt task, this
task is less ambiguous. In particular, since the premature recording of sales can be taken
care of by appropriate cut-off procedures at year end, the weakness related to the
improper credit authorization may appear to be the more urgent task requiring immediate
attention during the interim audit. Awareness of this aspect provides a cue for all
auditors (managers, top seniors, and mediocre seniors), and makes it easier for them to
anticipate the choices made by other auditors for this task. As a result, the knowledge
advantage of top seniors and managers over mediocre seniors (and of managers over top
seniors) is reduced, and we expect smaller differences in the predictive accuracy across
all groups of auditors:
H3: In terms of predicting the choices made by other auditors, there will be smaller
differences in the relative performance of managers, top seniors, and mediocre
seniors in the low-ambiguity task than in the high-ambiguity task.

3. Method
3.1 SUBJECTS
Our subjects consist of 28 audit seniors (mean experience=36 months) and 14
audit managers (mean experience=84 months) from a Big Five firm. We pair each
manager with two seniors with whom he/she normally works. The firm considers one
senior to be outstanding and the other to be mediocre (we call them ‘top’ and ‘mediocre’
seniors). The training manager (under the guidance of the staff partner) chose fourteen
pairs of top and mediocre seniors based on their overall performance, subject to the
condition that each top-mediocre pair must have worked for a particular manager.2 This
matching scheme enables each top/mediocre senior pair to make predictions about the
same manager, thereby controlling for variations in the managers’ choices. The
matching of a manager with only two seniors (as opposed to many other seniors) also
increases the likelihood that the matched managers and seniors are familiar with each
other.
3.2 PROCEDURE
At the start of the experiment, we inform audit seniors (managers) about the
name(s) of the specific manager (seniors) with whom they have been paired. To elicit
candid responses, we assure them that their choices and predictions will remain
confidential (Kuran [1995]). To reinforce this point, we instruct subjects not to write
down their names. The training manager distributed the instruments (with subject codes
known only to the training manager but not the researchers) directly to the subjects, and
the completed instruments are returned to the researchers.
10

Subjects perform two tasks (a bad-debt task and a control-weakness task) in
counterbalanced order. In the bad-debt task, they decide whether to permit a footnote
disclosure about the slow cash collection from the sales of a new product, or to insist on
an additional allowance for doubtful debts. In the control-weakness task, they decide
which of two internal control weaknesses (premature recording of sales and improper
credit authorization) discovered during the interim audit requires immediate follow-up.
For each task, subjects first choose an option, and then predict the choice(s) of
the auditor(s) with whom they have been paired. They also predict the aggregate number
of other seniors and managers participating in the study who will choose a particular
option. We tell the seniors (managers) that there are twenty-seven (twenty-six) other
seniors and thirteen other managers participating in the study. For each of the
predictions, subjects also indicate their confidence in their judgment on an eleven-point
scale ranging from 0 (very low confidence) to 10 (very high confidence).
Upon completion of the prediction tasks, subjects respond to questions relating
to our manipulation checks. To determine if subjects correctly perceive the control-
weakness task and the bad-debt task to be of low and high ambiguity, we ask subjects to
respond to two questions: (a) the extent to which they are confident that the control-
weakness task and the bad-debt task each has a right/wrong answer (1=very confident
that there is no right/wrong answer; 9=very confident that there is a right/wrong answer),
and (b) the extent to which they find each task to be complex (1=not at all complex;
9=extremely complex). We anticipate that, since the bad-debt task has more conflicting
issues than the control-weakness task, subjects will be less confident that the bad-debt
11

task has a correct answer, and will perceive the bad-debt task as more complex than the
control-weakness task.
To assess the degree to which subjects are familiar with each other, we ask them
to indicate the percentage of assignments and amount of time they have spent working
with the auditors with whom they have been paired, and how well they know the
working style and preferences of their paired auditors (1=do not know at all; 9=know
extremely well). Subjects also respond to other background and demographic questions.

4.2 MANIPULATION CHECKS
In this sub-section, we report the results of our manipulation checks. Subjects
indicate that they have less confidence that the bad-debt task has a right/wrong answer

than the control-weakness task (means=4.60 and 5.70; p=.009). They also find the bad-

Top and mediocre seniors report spending about 43% and 40% of their time

their paired managers are 6.32 and 6.58 (p=.581); managers’ ratings for the top and
mediocre seniors are 6.68 and 6.38 (p=.209).
5. CONCLUSION
This study investigates whether auditors can accurately predict the choices made
by their superiors and subordinates. Our results show that top seniors and managers are
no more accurate than mediocre seniors in predicting the choices of a specific superior or
subordinate for both low and high-ambiguity tasks. These results are consistent with the
findings in Gibbins and Newton [1994], who survey auditors and find that a substantial
proportion of their respondents indicate that they do not know the preferences of the
specific agent to whom they are accountable.
We find that for a high-ambiguity task, top seniors are more accurate than
mediocre seniors in predicting the aggregate choices (i.e., the number of seniors and
managers making a specific choice) of other managers (their superiors) and other seniors
(their peers). Managers, in turn, are more accurate than top seniors in predicting the
aggregate choices of other managers (their peers) and other seniors (their subordinates).
16

For a low-ambiguity task, there are no performance differences among managers, top
seniors, and mediocre seniors, implying that mediocre seniors’ lower sensitivity to the
preferences of other auditors may be mitigated when the task provides a cue on the likely
option chosen by other auditors. Overall, these results suggest that, under some
circumstances, managers and top seniors are better than mediocre seniors in predicting
the choices made by other auditors in their firm, and provide some limited support for
models of strategic auditor behavior (Rich, Solomon, and Trotman [1997]). Our results
also indicate that the ability to predict the choices made by other auditors is a dimension
of auditor expertise.
For the high-ambiguity task, the lack of differences among managers, top
seniors, and mediocre seniors in terms of how well they can predict individual choices is
somewhat surprising, given that we find significant differences for their predictive
accuracy of aggregate choices. We obtain this result for the predictions of a specific
paired auditor even though our participants have worked together for a period of time in
an environment with opportunities and incentives to interact and obtain feedback. One
possible interpretation of our results is that assessing the choices made by a specific
individual in a specific context is a complex task. If an individual’s preferences vary
across contexts and/or time (Tversky and Simonson [1993]), it may be difficult for
auditors to predict the other auditor’s choices in a different context, or over time. It is
also possible that our tests lack power because of our relatively small sample size.
The following limitations of our study should also be noted. First, while our
measure of aggregate choice captures the option chosen by a majority of the auditors
working independently, it does not necessarily reflect the choices made by auditors
17

interacting in a group setting. Such choices made in an interacting group setting may
depend on the group’s structure, distribution of power, and culture, which are likely to
add complexity to the task of predicting choices made in such settings (Gibbins and
Swieringa [1995]). We do not test the extent to which auditors can predict choices in
these contexts. Second, we do not assess seniors’ ability to predict the choices made by
their subordinates (staff auditors), or managers’ ability to predict the choices made by
their superiors (partners). These issues provide opportunities for future research.
