# Visual Style Guide

> L6 visual-style analysis. The corpus arrives as journal PDFs and SSRN manuscripts converted to Markdown. Publisher logos, blank image slots, and JSTOR or Wiley download banners are furniture. They were opened and then excluded. The patterns below come from pages and figures that carry the argument, sampled from the jargon, fair-value, data-analytics, readability, CSR, internal-control, tone, risk-disclosure, workload, justification, and older JAR papers.

## Image Strategy

### How many, and where

Most of the argument is a table. A paper can have no figure and still be complete. When figures appear, they are few, and they sit at two moments: the hypothesis (what the theory allows) and the result (what the cells did). They are not evenly spaced down the article, and there is no cover image. Working papers leave a bracketed line in the text, "[Insert Table 1 and Figure 1 about here]," and park the exhibit at the end or on the next page. Journal pages put the table or figure near the callout, often at the top or bottom of a column.

Older JAR conversions embed a raster of the whole page. Those rasters include a running header and, once, a JSTOR mark. They are the page, not an illustration. Do not add a logo, a portrait, or a decorative rule because a conversion captured one.

### Types that carry weight

Sampled figures, by function:

| Function | What was on the page | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prediction | Two lines, categorical x-axis, one line solid and one dashed. A shaded wedge when the theory only bounds a cell. Caption says "theoretical prediction" or "hypotheses." Note says the shade is a range of means, not a point. | Inspection risk × mindset. Mandatory disclosure × mandatory audit, panel A. |
| Result | The same axes, with cell means marked, sometimes the number printed at the point. A second panel splits a third factor (sophistication, material versus immaterial CSR). | Tone × readability, split by investor sophistication. CSR explicit-assessment × valence. Internal-control regimes, panel B. |
| Process | Boxes and one-way arrows. Coefficient, p-value, and tail written on each link. Indirect effect and a 90 percent confidence interval under the diagram. | Jargon → understanding, premium, or unnecessary-jargon → investment willingness, one panel per knowledge group. |
| Measurement | A left-to-right process with the variable names underneath. | Workload paper: planning, quarterly review, year-end fieldwork, and the hour variables that fall in each stage. Blue chevrons, gray boxes. |
| Stimulus | The instrument the participant saw. A hedge-versus-not-hedge earnings table with one line chart under it, or a CSR scorecard. Bold inside the stimulus marks the phrase the manipulation turned on. | Fair-value accounting-impact exhibit. |
| Table, which does more work than the figure | Means (SD) [n], then ANOVA with Type III sums of squares, then planned contrasts. Stars are defined in the note. | Readability understanding table. This is the default exhibit. |

No photographs of people, no memes, no stock illustrations, no word clouds. A university seal and a Pergamon device appear on a title page of the 2000 paper. They are the publisher's. Leave them out of anything you make.

### Prose and image, divided

The sentence before the exhibit tells the reader what comparison to look at. The exhibit holds the numbers or the shape. The note under the exhibit restates the coding and the pattern in one to four lines, including "all p-values are two-tailed unless noted with *." The paragraph after the exhibit says the pattern in a sentence that could stand if the exhibit were removed. Cell means are in the prose as well as in the table. A figure is not the only place a result exists.

Predicted figures are not drawn to look like results. The prediction can be a band. The result is points. Do not plot a predicted mean you did not predict.

### Color

Functional and plain. A solid blue line and a dashed orange or red line are enough. Green appears when a third series is required. The workload schematic uses a blue header bar and gray body, which is a template look, not a palette to spread through the paper. Body text is black. Axes are black. There is no gradient, no shadow, and no brand color. Excel-default markers (diamond, square, circle) are acceptable and were in fact used. If a journal later restyles the figure, the information to preserve is: two conditions on the x-axis, one line per level of the second factor, a legend that uses the condition's name, and a note that defines the dependent variable.

## Layout and Typography

### The page

Two production forms appear, and a new manuscript should follow the working-paper form unless a journal has already typeset it.

- SSRN manuscript: one column, serif, first-line or block paragraphs, footnotes at the bottom of the page, page number centered, a running footer that is the repository's ("Electronic copy available at..."). You do not add that footer. It is not a stylistic choice.
- Journal page: two columns, a running header with the author's name and the journal, footnotes in a smaller size at the column foot. The 2000 AOS paper and the JAR papers of 1995, 1997, and 1999 look like this. Do not imitate two-column layout in a manuscript.

There is no pull quote, no shaded box around a finding, and no sidebar. The only shaded region on a results page is inside a figure, and it means a range of predicted means.

### Headings

Numbered. Short. "1. Introduction." "III. EXPERIMENT" appears in one manuscript; Arabic numbering is more common. The next level is "4.1. Hypothesis Testing" or an unnumbered bold line, "Participants," "Manipulation checks," "Performance Judgment." One to six words. A heading is not a sentence and does not contain the result.

### Paragraphs

On the rendered pages, a paragraph is four to eight sentences, indented or set off by space, never one sentence except for the hypothesis paragraph and sometimes the roadmap. The measured median in the converted text is five sentences and about 160 words. Write to that, not to a social-media line break.

### Emphasis

Bold is for the hypothesis label, the research-question label, the table title, and a word inside a stimulus. Italics, where they appear, are for a journal title in the references or for a lettered variable in a sentence. Neither is used to raise the volume of an ordinary claim. Underlining is absent. All caps appear in a table banner ("HEDGE," "NOT HEDGE") and in a poorly converted scorecard, not in the prose.

### Lists

A limitation sequence is "First," "Second," inside paragraphs, not a bulleted list. A set of proxies can be numbered inside a sentence: "(1) ... (2) ... (3) ..." A sample-selection table is a table, not a list of bullets. The corpus does not use decorative bullets in the argument.

### Notes and appendix

Footnotes are short and subordinate: a coding choice, a citation that would break the sentence, the tail convention, an IRB or participant-pool detail. Appendices hold the instrument, the variable definitions, and a worked example of the manipulation. They are labeled Appendix A, B, C in the order the text cites them. The main text does not depend on the reader having opened them for the result. It does depend on them for replication.

## Color and Emphasis

Body color is black on white. Links, in the repository cover pages, are the repository's blue and are not part of the article. Do not color a word to mean "this is the finding."

The only color system to copy is the figure system:

- One hue per experimental condition, held constant across the prediction figure and the results figure.
- Solid versus dashed is the preferred second encoding, so the figure still works in grayscale. Several sampled figures use both hue and dash. Keep that redundancy.
- Red or orange is acceptable for a second line. It is not a code for "bad."
- A blue shade means "the theory allows this region." Do not shade a results chart.
- Significance stars are black, defined once: * 10 percent, ** 5 percent, *** 1 percent, or whatever the paper declares. Do not change the meaning of a star mid-paper. Experiments often prefer a printed p-value to a star. Archival tables use stars and also say "t-statistics in parentheses."

Callout boxes, highlight pens, and colored table rows are not in the corpus. A table distinguishes a total row with a rule or with bold type, not with a fill, except inside a stimulus exhibit where a column of net income may be lightly tinted to show the participant where to look. That tint is part of the manipulation. Do not tint a regression table.

### What "looks like" this corpus if the words were removed

A serif page, a short numbered heading, a dense paragraph, a parenthetical citation, a table with Panel A descriptives and Panel B the test, and, sometimes, a two-line interaction plot with a note underneath. White background. No portrait, no pull quote, no logo of the author.
