Enhancing Auditors’ Reliance on Data Analytics under Inspection Risk Using Fixed and
Growth Mindsets
ABSTRACT: Audit firms have invested significantly in data analytics (DA). However, evidence
shows that auditors are often reluctant to rely on DA. A major auditor concern is that inspectors
will second-guess the audit evidence gathered using DA. Drawing on psychology research, we
examine how the effect of inspection risk on auditors’ reliance on DA is moderated by a fixed
mindset (a belief that one’s ability is fixed) versus a growth mindset (a belief that one’s ability is
malleable). In an experiment with Big Four auditors as participants, we find that, relative to low
inspection risk, high inspection risk reduces auditors’ reliance on DA when auditors are prompted
with a fixed mindset, but increases it when auditors are prompted with a growth mindset. Our
findings contribute to auditing literature on DA, inspection risk, and mindsets, and have
implications for auditors, audit firms, and regulators.
Keywords: data analytics; inspection risk; mindsets; auditor judgment

I. INTRODUCTION
Audit firms across the globe have invested billions of dollars into new technologies such as
data analytics (DA) tools, which potentially improve audit quality by enabling auditors to perform
traditional audit procedures more efficiently and to perform innovative audit procedures that are
more effective (Bloomberg Tax 2020; Deloitte 2016; Raphael 2017). In fact, many audit firms
believe that DA will fundamentally change how auditors conduct audits (e.g., EY 2015; KPMG
2017). At the same time, auditors are facing elevated pressure of regulatory inspections (e.g.,
Johnson, Keune, and Winchel 2019; Westermann, Cohen, and Trompeter 2019).1 Inspections
potentially hinder auditors’ use of DA as auditors are concerned about inspectors’ second-guessing
of audit evidence gathered using DA (e.g., Gepp, Linnenluecke, O’Neill, and Smith 2018).2 For
example, one auditor interviewee in Austin, Carpenter, Christ, and Nielson (2021, 36) states,
“There may be fear that you’d get punished for doing the analytics because it doesn’t tie to the
standards… The firm is pushing the use of analytics, but there is still hesitation because of impact
on inspections.” Auditors’ limited use of DA may impair not only the return of audit firms’
investment in DA but also audit quality (Austin et al. 2021; Cao, Chychyla, and Stewart 2015).
Another factor that influences auditors’ use of DA relates to auditors’ beliefs about their
abilities to adapt and be conversant with this new technology. Individuals often have concerns

Psychology research on implicit theories suggests that individuals who believe or are led to believe
that their attributes are fixed or malleable have different goals, and these fixed or growth mindsets
shape their reactions to challenges and setbacks (Dweck and Leggett 1988; Yeager et al. 2019).
Individuals with a fixed mindset adopt a performance goal, in which they are concerned with
gaining (avoiding) favorable (unfavorable) judgments of their abilities (Elliott and Dweck 1988).
In contrast, those with a growth mindset adopt a learning goal, in which they seek to improve their
abilities via expending effort and learning from setbacks (Elliott and Dweck 1988). While there
are dispositional variations in these mindsets across individuals, these mindsets can also be
induced via behavioral interventions (Burnette, O’Boyle, VanEpps, Pollack, and Finkel 2013). The
challenges and potential negative feedback associated with inspections provide a pertinent real-
world setting for us to examine how induced fixed and growth mindsets moderate the effects of
inspection risk on auditors’ reliance on DA.
High inspection risk creates an accountability pressure that motivates auditors to increase
audit effort (Bhaskar 2020; Detzen, Gold, and Wright 2020; Stefaniak, Houston, and Brandon
2017). Moreover, auditors may perceive that their performance as a result of DA use is more likely
to be questioned when inspection risk is higher. Auditors prompted with a fixed mindset will be
more concerned that using DA has consequences that threaten their adequacy in a high-inspection-
risk (versus a low-inspection-risk) environment. Hence, they will be less likely to use DA as
inspection risk increases. In contrast, auditors prompted with a growth mindset have a learning

goal of enhancing their abilities, and they view effort and ability as positively related. When
inspection risk is higher, they will view this more demanding audit environment, wherein they
have to exert more effort to use DA successfully, as a better opportunity to achieve their learning
goal. Therefore, they will be more likely to use DA as inspection risk increases, compared to
auditors with a fixed mindset. As high inspection risk enhances (hinders) the use of DA by auditors
prompted with a growth (fixed) mindset, we expect that auditors prompted with a growth mindset
will be more likely to use DA than those prompted with a fixed mindset when inspection risk is
high. When inspection risk is low, it is unclear, ex ante, whether the elevated performance goal
associated with a fixed mindset operating in a low performance-risk environment, or the reduced
learning goal associated with a growth mindset in this same low performance-risk environment
will be more motivating. Hence, we do not make a directional prediction for mindset effects in this
situation.
To examine our research question, we conduct a 2 × 3 between-participants experiment with
inspection risk and mindset as the independent variables. In the experiment, Big Four auditors
assume the role of an audit manager planning the financial statement audit of a hypothetical client.
We manipulate inspection risk at two levels (high vs. low). In the (high-) low-risk condition, we
tell participants that, based on their experience, there is a (high) low likelihood that the regulator
will inspect their current audit engagement. We manipulate mindset at three levels: fixed, growth,
and control. Following psychology studies, we manipulate mindset by having participants read an
article that cues either a fixed mindset or a growth mindset. The fixed-mindset (growth-mindset)

article reports that individuals’ general ability is stable (malleable). The articles are unrelated to
auditing, ensuring a clean test of the theoretical effects. Participants who are assigned to the control
condition receive no article about the stability of an individual’s ability. As for the main dependent
variable, we ask participants to choose between two possible audit approaches to perform. One is
an innovative audit approach that requires using a DA tool to perform an Internet-based test, and
the other is a more traditional audit approach that requires using Microsoft Excel to perform an
offline test.
Consistent with our predictions, we find an interaction effect between inspection risk and
mindset. Relative to low inspection risk, high inspection risk decreases the use of DA by auditors
prompted with a fixed mindset but increases the use of DA by auditors prompted with a growth
mindset. Additional tests find that, when inspection risk is high, auditors prompted with a growth
mindset are more likely to use DA than those prompted with a fixed mindset or those receiving no
mindset prompt. When inspection risk is low, auditors prompted with a growth mindset are no
more likely to use DA than those receiving no mindset prompt. Interestingly, they are less likely
to use DA than those prompted with a fixed mindset. Results of our mediation analysis show that
the growth-mindset prompt helps to offset the negative impact of high inspection risk on DA use
by triggering learning-related thoughts and curbing challenge-related thoughts. At the same time,
the fixed-mindset prompt motivates auditors to use DA when inspection risk is low by triggering
them to demonstrate their abilities in a less challenging environment, but this motivating effect is
weaker when inspection risk increases.

Our study contributes to the literature on audit inspections by providing theory and evidence
on how high inspection risk can adversely influence auditors’ use of new technologies and
innovative audit procedures, and how a growth mindset can mitigate this effect. Interview evidence
suggests that auditors perceive that their hesitation to rely on DA is associated with the lack of
standards and fear of inspections (Austin et al. 2021). We provide causal evidence that the
demanding environment of high inspection risk discourages auditors with a fixed mindset from
using DA, but actually motivates auditors with a growth mindset to use DA. This finding suggests
that the effects of regulatory inspections on auditors’ reliance on DA are more nuanced than
previously expected. Views expressed by auditors in interviews (Austin et al. 2021) are consistent
with the reactions of auditors with a fixed mindset, while we show that auditors with a growth
mindset respond to regulatory inspections in a notably different way. Moreover, existing research
offers no solutions to auditors’ reluctance to use new audit technologies and procedures under
different levels of inspection risk. We add to this line of research by offering insights into
mechanisms that effectively motivate auditors to use DA under a high (low) level of inspection
risk, namely a growth (fixed) mindset.
In addition, we complement prior studies on auditor mindsets. Griffith, Hammersley, Kadous,
and Young (2015) demonstrate how a deliberative-mindset intervention improves auditor
performance in the context of complex estimates. Relatedly, Bauer, Hillison, Peecher, and
Pomeroy (2020) show that during audit planning, auditors hold an implemental mindset when
making decisions for themselves but a deliberative mindset when advising others. We extend this

II. BACKGROUND AND HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT
Auditors’ Reliance on DA
DA is relatively new to the audit profession as it has not been widely implemented by audit
firms or covered by audit textbooks until the last few years (Janvrin and Watson 2017; McKinney,
Yoos II, and Snead 2017). DA is believed to improve both audit quality and audit efficiency (e.g.,
Brown-Liburd, Issa, and Lombardi 2015; PCAOB 2017). For example, auditors can employ DA
to examine the entire population of transactions, narrow the scope of audit risky areas, identify and
flag anomalies and frauds, and collect new formats of audit evidence (Cao et al. 2015; Deloitte
2016; Raphael 2017).3 However, recent interview and survey studies suggest that many auditors
hesitate to utilize DA despite its benefits (Eilifsen, Kinserdal, Messier, and McKee 2020; Gepp et
al. 2018). Factors that hinder auditors’ use of DA are not well researched. One such factor is
regulators’ inspections (e.g., Austin et al. 2021).

Fixed and Growth Mindsets
Implicit theories posit that people may hold one of two possible types of beliefs about human
attributes (e.g., Dweck 2016; Dweck and Leggett 1988). One type of belief is that human attributes
such as abilities and intelligence are fixed traits that cannot be developed. People with this belief
have a fixed mindset. The other type of belief is that human attributes are malleable qualities that
can be developed. People with this belief have a growth mindset. The mindsets and their associated
beliefs can occur naturally, or be changed by the environment or by interventions (e.g., Burnette
et al. 2013).
People with different mindsets have different goal orientations. Those with a fixed mindset
believe that their attributes such as abilities cannot grow by expending effort or receiving help
from others. They tend to attribute successes to adequacy of their abilities and failures to
inadequacy of their abilities. This belief causes them to pursue a performance goal, in which they
seek to establish the adequacy of their abilities and to avoid giving evidence of their inadequacy
(Dweck and Leggett 1988). In contrast, those with a growth mindset believe that their attributes
8

such as abilities can grow if they expend more effort or learn from setbacks. That is, they tend to
attribute successes (failures) to their (lack of) effort, rather than their (lack of) abilities. Their belief
leads them to pursue a learning goal, in which they seek to enhance their abilities via learning
(Dweck and Leggett 1988).
Different mindsets and their related goal orientations alter how people interpret and react to
inputs and outcomes of events (Elliott and Dweck 1988; Hong, Chiu, Dweck, Lin, and Wan 1999).
Individuals with a fixed mindset (and thus a performance goal) view effort and ability as negatively
related and outcomes as measures of abilities. For them, high effort and failures signify a lack of
inherent abilities. As a result, they tend to exhibit a behavioral pattern characterized by avoidance
of challenges and deterioration of performance in the face of challenges (e.g., Dweck and Leggett
1988). In contrast, individuals with a growth mindset (and thus a learning goal) view effort and
ability as positively related and outcomes as measures of learning effectiveness. For them, high
effort and failures signify growth in abilities. As a result, they tend to exhibit a mastery-oriented
behavioral pattern, which is characterized by pursuits of challenging tasks and persistent striving
in the face of failures (Elliott and Dweck 1988). In fact, a more challenging context, where they
have to expend more effort to succeed, offers a better opportunity for them to learn (Dweck and
Leggett 1988).6

Extant psychology research demonstrates mindset effects in a variety of settings. For
example, growth-mindset interventions improve students’ academic performance, especially for
disadvantaged students (Claro, Paunesku, and Dweck 2016; Yeager et al. 2019). In a longitudinal
study, Burnette and Finkel (2012) show that a growth-mindset intervention helps dieters manage
their body weight after dieting setbacks. Nussbaum and Dweck (2008) show that individuals who
endorse a fixed (growth) mindset are more likely to engage in downward (upward) social
comparisons. In a business-like setting, Kray and Haselhuhn (2007) demonstrate that negotiators’
belief about whether one’s negotiation ability is fixed or malleable alters their negotiation behavior
and outcome. Specifically, the authors find that most of the participants who are put in a fixed
mindset choose to show off their negotiation skills at the cost of not learning any new negotiation
skills, while most of the participants who are put in a growth mindset choose to learn rather than
show off negotiation skills at the cost of easily making mistakes and getting confused. They also
find that, when participants actually negotiate, those with a growth mindset perform better (i.e.,
claim more resources) in negotiations than those with a fixed mindset.
Joint Effect of Inspection Risk and Mindset on Auditors’ Reliance on DA
We posit that inspection risk and auditors’ mindset jointly impact auditors’ reliance on DA.
As inspection risk increases, audit failures are more likely to be documented, publicized, and
punished. To mitigate the negative consequences associated with publicized audit failures, auditors
exert more effort to improve audit quality as inspection risk increases (e.g., Bhaskar 2020; Detzen
et al. 2020). Here, auditors with a fixed mindset will be more concerned that they have to expend

significant effort in using DA to ensure high audit quality and to pass regulatory inspections. Both
escalated effort and heightened possibility of publicized performance failures associated with the
use of DA are indications of low ability for these auditors. With higher costs of performance
failures in this high-inspection-risk environment, using DA to achieve their performance goal of
appearing competent becomes a less attractive option for these auditors (e.g., Dweck and Leggett
1988). Hence, we posit the following hypothesis:
H1: High, as opposed to low, inspection risk will reduce the use of DA by auditors prompted
with a fixed mindset.
In contrast, the high audit-quality requirement in a high-inspection-risk environment is more
motivating for auditors with a growth mindset to learn and use DA. This is because they perceive
settings where they have to expend more effort to succeed as better opportunities to achieve their
learning goal (Elliott and Dweck 1988). For example, Job, Dweck, and Walton (2010) find that
individuals induced to hold a growth mindset about willpower perform worse after a task that does
not result in ego depletion than after a task that does, suggesting that growth mindset is more
motivating when the situation is more challenging. Likewise, Burnette et al. (2013) show that ego
threat (i.e., any event or communication having unfavorable implications about the self) magnifies
the benefit of growth mindset over fixed mindset. Finally, Abernethy, Anderson, Nair, and Jiang
(2021) show that primary and secondary school principals who endorse a growth mindset conduct
more fundraising activities than those who endorse a fixed mindset, but this mindset effect is
observed only from low-performance schools with greater challenges, and not from high-

performance schools. With the amplified motivating effect of the learning goal under the
demanding environment of high inspection risk, auditors with a growth mindset will be more likely
to use DA when inspection risk increases. We state our second hypothesis as follows:
H2: High, as opposed to low, inspection risk will increase the use of DA by auditors prompted
with a growth mindset.
The discussion above suggests that the demanding environment of high inspection risk
motivates auditors with a growth mindset to use DA, whereas it discourages auditors with a fixed
mindset from using DA.7 This divergence in mindset effects implies that auditors prompted with
a growth mindset will be more likely to use DA than auditors prompted with a fixed mindset when
inspection risk is high. This prediction is consistent with a large body of psychology research
documenting that growth mindset helps individuals thrive under challenging scenarios (e.g., Claro
et al. 2016). Accordingly, our third hypothesis is:
H3: Auditors prompted with a growth mindset will be more likely to use DA than auditors
prompted with a fixed mindset when inspection risk is high.

participants.9 We exclude 29 participants who give inconsistent responses to our dependent

Design
Our experiment employs a 2 × 3 between-participants design with inspection risk (high vs.
low) and mindset (fixed vs. growth vs. control) as the independent variables. We ask participants
to assume the role of an audit manager, planning the financial statement audit of a hypothetical
client (ABC Inc.). Participants begin the experiment by learning the background of ABC Inc. and
its historical financial information. Participants also receive pre-audit financial statements for the
current fiscal year. Next, participants are exposed to the manipulations of inspection risk and
mindset. After receiving the manipulations, participants are asked to answer our dependent
measures, followed by manipulation checks, questions of process measures, and demographic
questions. Participants complete the study in Chinese. We cross-translate the instrument between
English and Chinese to ensure equivalency. Partners of the participating audit firm reviewed the
instrument, and we made minor changes following their suggestions before we conduct the
experiment.
Independent Variables
We manipulate inspection risk at two levels (high vs. low). In the (high-) low-risk condition,
we tell participants that, based on their experience, there is a (high) low likelihood that the regulator
will inspect their current audit engagement. To prevent participants from inferring the levels of
15
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3850527

audit risk based on the levels of inspection risk, we explicitly inform participants that the audit risk
of the current engagement is moderate in all conditions.
We manipulate mindset at three levels: fixed, growth, and control. We adopt a well-
established approach of manipulating mindset from the psychology literature (e.g., Job et al. 2010;
Kray and Haselhuhn 2007; O’Keefe, Dweck, and Walton 2018). Specifically, we ask participants
to read a short article intended to prompt a fixed mindset or a growth mindset, depending on
condition. Participants in the fixed-mindset condition read an article titled “Ability, Like Plaster,
is Pretty Stable over Time.” The article states, “While it used to be believed that ability could be
developed throughout the lifetime, experts in the field now believe that people possess a finite set
of rather fixed ability.” Participants in the growth-mindset condition read an article titled “Ability
is Changeable and Can be Developed.” This article states, “While it used to be believed that ability
was a fixed attribute that people were either born with or not, experts in the field now believe that
ability is a dynamic attribute that can be cultivated and developed over a lifetime.”
Importantly, this manipulation is unrelated to the audit task of our experiment, and the
articles mention no information about DA or inspections. This design feature allows us to cleanly
manipulate auditor mindset at the construct level without introducing confounding priming or
demand effects, ensuring the internal validity of our study (Griffith, Kadous, and Young 2016).
Lastly, we include a control condition wherein participants receive no article about the stability of
individuals’ ability. Without mindset prompts, auditors’ use of DA is influenced by their
dispositional mindsets.

Task and Dependent Variables
After receiving the manipulations, all participants are given a table describing the
implementation details and costs of two possible (one DA, one traditional) approaches for auditing
the revenues of ABC Inc. This table is held constant across conditions. The two approaches are
both analytical procedures, and their underlying objective is identical: to identify an abnormal
trend in sales. However, they differ in two ways. First, the DA approach requires auditors to use a
DA tool to analyze the client’s sales transactions and identify anomalies by comparing the client’s
sales figures with Google Trends Index values for the client’s potential customers’ searches of its
key products. In contrast, in the traditional approach, auditors use Excel to analyze the client’s
sales transactions and identify anomalies by comparing the client’s sales figures with industry-
trend information published in local economic journals. Second, we inform participants that, in the
DA approach, they can use the DA tool to analyze all or a sample of journal entries related to sales.
In the traditional approach, they can select a sample of entries from the sales journal and cross-
check them with sales invoices.
Our descriptions capture a number of key differences between DA and traditional audit
approaches as discussed in the academic literature (e.g., Cao et al. 2015; Earley 2015) and
practitioners’ publications (e.g., Deloitte 2016): (1) whether a computer-assisted audit tool
(CAAT) is used; (2) whether a novel source of data from the Internet is analyzed; and (3) whether
full-population testing is enabled. In terms of costs, the DA approach requires familiarity in the

V. CONCLUSION
We experimentally investigate how the impact of inspection risk on auditors’ reliance on DA
is moderated by auditors’ mindsets. In our experiment, we give auditors either a fixed-mindset
prompt telling them that people’s ability is fixed or a growth-mindset prompt telling them that
people’s ability is malleable. Our main results suggest that, relative to low inspection risk, high
inspection risk reduces auditors’ reliance on DA when auditors are prompted with a fixed mindset,
but increases it when auditors are prompted with a growth mindset. We further show that when
inspection risk is high, auditors prompted with a growth mindset are more likely to use DA than
those prompted with a fixed mindset. In contrast, auditors prompted with a growth mindset are
less likely to use DA than those prompted with a fixed mindset when inspection risk is low.
Our study contributes to the accounting literature in several ways. First, prior research has
raised a concern that regulatory inspections curb auditors’ adoption of innovative audit procedures
(Kang et al. 2020). We show that this is true for auditors with a fixed mindset. However, high
inspection risk may promote the adoption of innovative audit procedures, such as DA, for auditors
with a growth mindset. This finding deepens our understanding of inspection outcomes by showing
that inspections may have a positive or a negative impact on auditors’ use of DA, depending on
their mindsets. Second, extant studies observe that auditors are less willing to rely on new
is lessened when inspection risk is low. They differ from the results of the main analysis in that fixed mindset does
not enhance auditors’ use of DA, relative to growth mindset, when inspection risk is low.

technologies than audit firms may have wished (e.g., Austin et al. 2021; Commerford, Dennis, Joe,
and Ulla 2020). Mechanisms that encourage auditors to do so have not been documented. Our
study fills this gap by showing that a growth (fixed) mindset effectively encourages auditors to

Two caveats potentially apply. First, we recruit participants from Taiwan, where the
international auditing standards are followed, but regulatory inspections may not be as intense as
in countries such as the U.S. In the current audit environment of the regimes with a high overall
level of inspection risk such as the U.S., our finding and implications about the growth-mindset
effect in the high-inspection-risk condition are arguably even more relevant. Second, we recruit
all participants from one Big Four firm. Different audit firms may have different cultures or norms
concerning the use of new technologies such as DA tools. Future studies may explore firm-level
moderators of the effects we show.

interventions that enhance auditors’ reliance on DA under different levels of inspection risk. We
employ a one-shot laboratory experiment where we use short articles to induce mindsets. While
our manipulation of mindset has been widely used in the psychology literature as a valid and
efficient way to test the theory, there is little empirical evidence on how long the effect of this

remotely via online videos and/or emails, and hence, they are cost-efficient.32
Audit firms should also consider situational factors and individual attributes when designing
and implementing mindset interventions. First, the consistency between firm culture and the
message conveyed in the mindset intervention is important. A growth-mindset intervention would
fit better with a type of firm culture that values learning and effort instead of raw talent. In contrast,

of the participating audit firm.

a fixed-mindset intervention would likely be more effective when the firm culture values
performance more than learning. When firm culture and the mindset intervention send conflicting
messages, auditors may be less receptive to the intervention.
Second, dispositional mindsets of auditors and their superiors matter. For example, audit
superiors with a growth (fixed) mindset are expected to cultivate their subordinates’ growth (fixed)
mindset more effectively than audit superiors without a growth (fixed) mindset (Heslin et al. 2005;
Heslin, VandeWalle, and Latham 2006). Auditors’ own dispositional mindsets also determine the
extent to which they benefit from a mindset intervention. Audit firms may consider measuring
auditors’ mindsets and customizing interventions based on auditors’ dispositional mindsets.
Third, auditors’ anticipated regulator tone likely matters. For example, while audit firms have
invested heavily in new technologies, the PCAOB has yet to offer clear guidance on DA use. One
can reasonably assume that auditors may not perceive a positive tone (or may even perceive a
negative tone) towards DA use from the PCAOB. A perception that regulators have a negative tone
towards DA use may create obstacles for a mindset intervention to work. We encourage regulators
and inspectors to consider the implications of our findings.
One interesting, and perhaps surprising, finding of our study is that auditors prompted with a
fixed mindset are more likely to use DA than auditors prompted with a growth mindset under low
inspection risk. Our coding of participants’ rationales provides one explanation for this finding:
auditors prompted with a fixed mindset are more eager to use DA to prove their abilities, whereas
auditors prompted with a growth mindset are less motivated to learn new systems when inspection

risk is lower. We also note that the effect of a fixed mindset under low inspection risk is more
pronounced when the mindset is manipulated than naturally assessed, which potentially reflects
the difference in the strength of dispositional mindset versus induced mindset. These findings
deepen our understanding of mindset effects and underscore the need for more research that
explores their moderators.
