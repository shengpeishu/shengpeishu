# Structure Patterns

> L2 article-structure analysis. The corpus supports three recurring structures. Sixteen papers are experiments (including two-experiment papers and one field measurement study that still uses the experimental skeleton). Three are archival. One is a review. Counts below are the structures as they appear, not a quota.

Every empirical paper in the corpus is a thesis, a body, and a conclusion. The schema label is thesis-body-conclusion. What changes is what the body is obliged to contain. Chronology, Q&A, and a comparison essay are not structures here. Comparison lives inside the design (a 2×2), not as the architecture of the article.

## Experiment

Used when the paper manipulates or measures a feature of a judgment and holds the economics, the case, or the number constant. This is the dominant structure: the preparer-justification, materiality, jargon, tone, readability, fair-value, guidance, CSR, data-analytics, accountability, memory, analyst-warning, internal-control, and "can auditors predict" papers, plus the field expertise paper where the "manipulation" is rank and the outcome is a real performance evaluation.

Reader expectation: by the end of the introduction they know the interaction, not just the topic. They expect a labeled hypothesis before any mean. They expect the conclusion to restate the condition, name who should care, and list limitations. They do not expect a surprise ending.

```
[Opening: regulatory or practical tension, or a prior result set against a conflicting result. 150–400 words. Not a scene, not a statistic.]
→ [First turn: "In this paper, we investigate..." The turn names the moderator that makes the prior result conditional.]
→ [Body: hypothesis development → method → results, in that order. Numbered sections.]
→ [Transition: end of introduction is a one- or two-sentence roadmap. Each later section opens by saying which hypothesis it addresses.]
→ [Close: restatement in words, implications for a named audience, numbered limitations, future research tied to each limitation.]
```

### Title

Two productive patterns.

- Effect title: "Effects of X on Y," or "The Joint Effect of X and Y on Z." Use this when both factors are already the contribution.
- Question or hook title: "Can Auditors Predict the Choices Made by Other Auditors?", "Who Likes Jargon?", "What Drives Investor Response to CSR Performance Reports?", "When the Use of Positive Language Backfires." The body still opens as an assertion. The question is not repeated as the first sentence.

A subtitle is allowed when it names the moderator the title left out: "How Does Readability Influence Investors' Judgments? Consistency of Benchmark Performance Matters."

### Abstract

120–220 words. Four moves, in order: what is manipulated or measured; the design in one clause ("in an experiment with Big Four auditors"); the interaction, with the condition named; one sentence on who the result informs. At most one citation. Keywords are a semicolon list of constructs, not of audiences. No roadmap in the abstract.

### Introduction

Five paragraphs is typical. Do not open with "In this paper."

1. The setting, in the institution's own terms. A standard, a filing rule, a workload fact, a press claim. One or two paragraphs.
2. The prior result, and the reason it is incomplete. "Prior research indicates that guidance form has no effect on earnings estimates. We extend this research by showing that guidance form matters, conditional on investment position and news valence."
3. "In this paper, we..." Design preview and the result preview. The result preview states the interaction. It may include the direction. It does not include F-statistics.
4. Contribution as boundary conditions on named streams. Two streams is the ordinary number. "In doing so, we extend the theory in each of these two research streams."
5. Roadmap.

Allowed variation: the result preview can move to the end of paragraph 3 if the design needs a sentence of its own. Not allowed: an anecdote that is not later the experimental case; a history of the literature longer than the gap it motivates.

### Hypothesis development

One subsection per construct, then a subsection for the interaction. Each subsection ends in a prediction or explains why a main effect is not signed. The hypothesis is a separate paragraph under a label. If the theory only signs an ordinal pattern, the prediction figure shows a shaded band of possible means, not a false-precise line. Say that the figure "depicts the possible patterns."

A moderator the theory does not sign becomes a research question, labeled RQ, not a fake hypothesis.

### Method

Order is stable: participants, design, independent variables, dependent variables, procedure. Variations that are allowed:

- A within-subjects factor gets its own short subsection.
- A second experiment gets "Experiment 2," with a shorter method that says only what changed.
- Exclusions are stated with the count and the reason ("29 participants who gave inconsistent responses").
- The dependent variable that is an average of two questions reports the correlation or Cronbach's alpha in the results, not as a boast in the method.

The stimulus is shown by one rewritten sentence ("for example, 'strong' and 'pleased' were replaced with neutral wording"), not by a tour of the appendix. The appendix can hold the full instrument.

### Results

Manipulation checks first, each with a mean difference and a p-value, and a sentence that says the manipulation succeeded. Then hypotheses in label order. For each: the test name, the pattern in words, the cell means with standard deviations, the p-value and its tail. A significant interaction is followed by simple effects. A null that the theory predicted is reported as a result, not hidden. Mediation, when the theory named a process, is a path: coefficient, one-tailed or two-tailed as precommitted, indirect effect, and a 90 percent confidence interval. Do not write "the mediator mattered" with no path.

Figures of the pattern sit here or are called from here with "[Insert Table 1 and Figure 1 about here]" in a working paper. The table note restates the coding. The figure note restates who is on which line.

### Closing

No new statistic that changes the claim. Three parts:

- Restate the conditional finding in one paragraph, in the same nouns as the hypothesis.
- Implications for one or two named readers: standard setters, a regulator, audit firms, managers. The implication says what the result changes in a decision, not that the topic is important.
- Limitations, numbered. The first is usually the specific stimulus or the specific participant pool. Each one says what is unknown and what a next study would vary. Stop. Do not rise to a slogan.

### Field variant

The expertise paper uses the same skeleton with two substitutions. Participants are auditors whose performance evaluations already exist. The "results" compare knowledge and ability measures across ranks against those evaluations. The closing still lists generalizability limits (one office, one country, a small manager sample) in the first person plural. Use this variant only when the outcome is a field measure. Do not costume a laboratory task as a field study.

## Archival

Used when the paper estimates an association on engagement or firm data and the contribution is a condition under which a previously mixed association appears. Three papers: non-audit fees and auditor specialization, busy-season workload and audit quality, engagement profitability. The profitability paper adds partner interviews in front of the archive; the interviews justify the proxy, they do not become oral history.

Reader expectation: several noisy proxies of one construct, an equation written out, and a coefficient interpreted in words. They expect the paper to say why prior studies disagree, and to answer with a moderator rather than with a new adjective for the same regression.

```
[Opening: a regulatory concern plus mixed prior evidence. 200–400 words.]
→ [First turn: the association is conditional on a feature prior papers did not separate. Name the feature.]
→ [Body: background and one interaction hypothesis → sample and proxies → equation → results and robustness.]
→ [Transition: "we use multiple proxies because each is noisy." Each results subsection names the proxy.]
→ [Close: shorter than an experiment. Implication for regulators or firms, then the limits of the proxies.]
```

### What changes relative to the experiment

- The introduction lists the proxies before the results. Going-concern opinions, accruals, benchmark beating, and earnings-response coefficients are introduced as a set, with one sentence on what each one can and cannot capture.
- Hypothesis development includes an institutional background subsection. There is usually one interaction hypothesis, not five.
- The method is "Data and research design." A sample-selection table (starts with, minus, final N) is part of the argument. The equation is displayed, then a "where" paragraph defines every variable in the order it appears.
- Results open with descriptives and correlations, then the regression. A coefficient is a direction and a comparison ("specialists versus nonspecialists"), not a naked Greek letter.
- Robustness is a subsection, not a footnote consisting of the word "robust." Alternative cutoffs, alternative fee metrics, and alternative specialist definitions each get a sentence on whether the interaction survives.
- Interviews, if any, are reported before the archive and are used to defend the dependent variable. Nine partners who agree on a proxy is a measurement claim. It is not a narrative.

### What does not change

"We find" is still the result move. "Consistent with" still needs a referent. Limitations are still numbered and still name the proxy's weakness. The paper still refuses a universal main effect.

## Review

Used once, for the 2005 judgment-and-decision-making review. Use it when the job is to re-sort a literature and point at gaps, not to test a new hypothesis.

Reader expectation: an explicit reason this taxonomy is different from the last review, then sections that end in what is missing. They do not expect new data. They do expect the gaps to be specific enough to start a paper.

```
[Opening: what this review covers, in one sentence that includes the method of the literature ("a psychological lens"). 100–200 words.]
→ [First turn: prior reviews are named, and the difference in categorization is stated. Not praised, not dismissed.]
→ [Body: a small number of buckets. Here, task, person, and interpersonal interaction. Inside each bucket: what has accumulated, then the gap.]
→ [Transition: the start of each bucket says why the bucket is a feature of the setting, not why it was convenient.]
→ [Close: future work tied to a change in the environment (new standards, a shift in practice), not a summary of every section.]
```

Allowed variation: the buckets can be other integral features of a setting. Three is the number this corpus actually uses. Not allowed: a review organized only as a chronology of papers, or a conclusion that lists gaps with no link to a change in practice.

The voice stays "we," including "we believe that this categorization provides a useful lens." Belief is allowed in a review when it is immediately tied to a feature of the setting. It is not allowed as a substitute for a result in an experiment.

## Shared transitions

Across all three types, the handoff is explicit and dull, on purpose.

- End of introduction: "The next section develops the hypotheses. The third section discusses the method. Results follow. The final section discusses implications and limitations."
- Start of a results subsection: "H2 predicts that..." then the test.
- Between experiments: "Given this result, Experiment 2 tests whether [a remedy] reduces the effect."
- Into limitations: "The following limitations should be noted."

Do not write a transition that evaluates the prose ("Now that we have explored..."). The section heading already did that work.
