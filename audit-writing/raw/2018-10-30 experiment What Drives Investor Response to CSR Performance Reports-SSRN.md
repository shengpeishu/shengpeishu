What Drives Investor Response to CSR Performance Reports?
ABSTRACT: Recent research finds that investors’ assessments of a stock’s fundamental value
are influenced by CSR performance through the affect-as-information heuristic (Elliott et al.
2014). We extend prior research by examining two boundary conditions for the use of this
heuristic: (1) whether the CSR performance relates to activities that are integrated in a firm’s
core business practices (material CSR issues) or not (immaterial CSR issues), and (2) whether
the CSR performance is positive or negative. Employing an experimental method, we find that
the affect-as-information heuristic applies only to immaterial CSR issues but not to material
CSR issues, and only to positive but not negative CSR performance. Our findings suggest that
investors likely use a heuristic approach to process immaterial and positive CSR issues, and a
more deliberate and systematic approach to process material or negative CSR issues. Our study
has both practical and theoretical implications.
Keywords: corporate social responsibility; material sustainability; core business activities;
unintentional judgments; systematic versus heuristic processing

1. Introduction
The adoption and implementation of corporate social responsibility (henceforth, CSR)
initiatives has generated a growing interest in both practice1 and academic research (De Graaf
and Slager 2009; Moser and Martin 2012; Krüeger 2015; Hales et al. 2016). There is
controversy on whether CSR performance factors should be systematically and explicitly
included into traditional financial analysis (McWilliams and Siegel 2001; Lev et al. 2010;
Petrovits 2006; Brammer and Millington 2008; Di Guili and Kostovetsky 2014).
Notwithstanding this debate, recent research (Elliott et al. 2014) has found that investors’
assessments of a stock’s fundamental value are influenced by CSR performance through the
affect generated from exposure to the valence of CSR performance. Specifically, this CSR
effect on fundamental value assessment is unintentional and attributable to the use of an affect-
as-information heuristic (Elliott et al. 2014; Schwarz and Clore 2007). This is heuristic
processing in that affect, a peripheral cue, is used to form a judgment, and is in contrast to
systematic, deliberate processing of the CSR information (Chaiken and Maheswaran 1994).
In this study, we build on prior research by explicitly considering whether this heuristic
processing of CSR performance (hereafter, CSR-heuristic effect) depends on (1) the nature of
the CSR activities, and (2) the valence of the CSR performance. In examining the nature of the
CSR activities, we focus on whether the activities directly relate to a firm’s core business, or
are peripheral and only remotely related to a firm’s core business. Following Hales et al. (2016),
we define a firm’s decision making to be socially responsible if the firm considers the impact

From a theoretical perspective, examining how the CSR-heuristic effect operates for
material versus immaterial CSR issues allows us to refine the theoretical mechanism associated
with this effect. The CSR-heuristic effect is posited to arise from the use of the affect-as-
information heuristic (Elliott et al. 2014), and psychology research recognizes that affect or
feelings “can serve as a basis of accurate as well as mistaken inferences, depending on the
relationship between the feeling and the target” (Schwarz and Clore 2007, 386). In the context
of our study, affect relates to that induced from exposure to the positive/negative CSR
performance, and the target relates to the fundamental value of the firm. We refine both
accounting and psychology literature by further introducing the notion of the relationship
between the target and the information upon which affect is formed as an important theoretical
link. That is, if the affect-inducing information is less related to the target, the use of affect-as-
information to make judgments about the target is less likely to be on an informed basis,
compared to one where the affect-inducing information is more related to the target. In the
context of our study, immaterial CSR issues are likely to provide information that is less
directly related to fundamental value assessments, while material CSR issues are likely to
provide information more directly related to fundamental value assessments. This latter
condition suggests that the effect of CSR performance on fundamental value assessments is
likely to be informed, which is different from what Elliott et al. (2014) indicate, but also
suggests the possibility of an alternative mechanism—investors directly use the relevant,
diagnostic information conveyed by material CSR issues to make fundamental value
assessments. Following Elliott et al. (2014), we use a theory-consistent mitigating
mechanism—prompting participants to explicitly assess CSR performance—to show that
investors’ judgments are likely to be unintentional if their affect-influenced judgments are not

We conducted a 2 (present vs. absent explicit assessment) × 2 (material vs. immaterial

MBA students, who took on the role of investors and estimated the fundamental value of a firm.
We adapted the research instrument materials of Elliott et al. (2014) and added the manipulation
of materiality of CSR issues to test our hypotheses. We manipulated the materiality of CSR
issues by creating two conditions. In the material CSR issue condition, participants were
provided with two salient material dimensions connected to core business practices (i.e.,
employees/supply chain and customers’ satisfaction). In the immaterial condition, the two
salient CSR dimensions were oriented towards non-core business practices (i.e., environmental
impact and green policies). We manipulated explicit assessment of CSR performance by either
prompting or not prompting participants to explicitly evaluate the firm’s CSR performance.
Finally, we manipulated the valence of CSR performance by providing participants with either
positive or negative CSR performance metrics.

fundamental value estimates are not affected by the presence or absence of an explicit
assessment of CSR. When a firm’s CSR performance is driven by immaterial CSR issues, an
explicit assessment of CSR performance removes the positive effect of CSR performance on
investors’ fundamental value assessments. We also find that the mitigating effect of explicit

We contribute to the CSR literature by providing theory and evidence that investors’
heuristic processing of CSR performance information in making fundamental value
assessments documented in prior research (Elliott et al. 2014) is conditional on two features of
CSR performance information—materiality and valence. Making inferences from affect
(derived from exposure to CSR performance) to fundamental value assessments, a form of
heuristic processing, can lead to erroneous judgments when the relation between the affect and
fundamental value assessments is tenuous.
Our findings have practical implications. Our evidence is consistent with the use of
deliberate and systematic (and reduced propensity to engage in heuristic) processing of
information for material CSR issues endorses the SASB’s initiative to build the Materiality
Map™ and should be helpful to the SASB in its promotion of the disclosure of material CSR
issues. Our paper also has implications to the integrated reporting campaign in recent years that
advocates for integrating CSR reports with traditional financial reporting (International
Integrated Reporting Council (IIRC) 2013). Perhaps of concern to the SASB and regulators is
our finding that investors likely process immaterial and positive CSR performance information
unintentionally—this implies that a firm can strategically influence investors’ perceptions of
its fundamental value by disclosing more immaterial CSR issues, and exaggerating the positive
performance in these areas.

Material and Immaterial CSR
Following Hales et al. (2016), we define a firm’s decision making as socially
responsible if the firm considers the impact of its activities on unrelated third parties (i.e.,
“externalities” in economic theories). Such impact can be either positive (e.g., charity donation
that benefits the community) or negative (e.g., air pollution that harms the entire society). “It
is the consideration of externalities that makes decision making ‘social,’ and it is the weights
on those factors that make the decision making ‘responsible” (Hales et al. 2016, 14). This
definition does not exclude core business-related activities from being considered as “socially
responsible” as long as these activities have an impact on unrelated third parties. Examples
include improved product quality and safety benefits for customers beyond the goods/service
that the customers have received through the transaction, and employee training programs that
enhance employees’ skills that they possess personally even when they no longer work for the
firm.
Nonetheless, these core business-related activities do differ from other peripheral
practices (such as donations) of a firm in that the former is more closely related to the firm’s
financial condition and operating performance than the latter. As a result, information regarding
the core business-related activities is more likely to be considered important for investment
judgments, and is therefore “material” (see the U.S. Supreme Court’s (1976) definition in
Footnote 3 we cited earlier). Therefore, we define issues that are directly related to the firm’s
core business practices as “material CSR issues,” and issues that are related to the firm’s
peripheral practices as “immaterial CSR issues.”
Recent research argues for a normative relationship between material CSR issues and
firm value. While material CSR issues directly contribute to a company’ core business and
improve its financial performance (Barnett 2007; Yuan et al. 2011), the pursuit of immaterial

CSR may distract managers from their core duties which, in turn, creates unnecessary costs,
including inefficient resource allocation; eventually, financial performance is undermined
(Wang and Bansal 2012). Material CSR issues improve a firm’s financial performance through
satisfying the interests of stakeholders who possess economic power that directly influences
the firm’s operating performance (Mitchell et al. 1997). Research in accounting provides
evidence consistent with this view. Khan et al. (2016) document that firms with strong ratings
on industry-specific material CSR issues outperform peers with poor ratings on these issues.
In an experiment with graduate students, Cheng et al. (2015) show that investors are more
willing to invest in the company when CSR reporting is more (versus less) relevant to the firm’s
business strategy.
When Does Heuristic Processing of CSR Information Occur?
In studying how CSR information affects investors’ valuation judgments, prior
research finds that affect from exposure to CSR performance carries over to investors’
fundamental value assessments (Elliott et al. 2014). The authors use the affect-as-information
theory, a heuristic process, to explain this finding. That is, when the individuals do not attribute
affect to its source (e.g., to the CSR report as opposed to the financial statements), their
subsequent judgments will be unintentionally influenced by their affective responses; however,
when people are prompted to attribute their affect to its source, their affective responses have
little influence on their subsequent judgments (Schwarz and Clore 1983, 2003, 2007; Clore and
Huntsinger 2007). Elliott et al. (2014) find evidence consistent with this account, with investors’
fundamental value assessments being more influenced by CSR performance valence in the
absence than presence of an explicit assessment of CSR performance. Elliott et al. (2014)
conclude that investors process CSR performance information in a heuristic manner through
the affect-as-information mechanism.

We refine the theoretical mechanism associated with the use of the affect-as-
information heuristic in processing CSR performance. Psychology research points out that the
use of affect can lead to either accurate or mistaken inferences, conditional on the relation
between the affect and the target (Schwarz and Clore 2007).4 In our setting, affect refers to the
sense of favorability towards the reporting company induced from exposure to the
positive/negative CSR performance, and the target refers to the fundamental value of the firm.
Extending both accounting and psychology literature, we posit that the relation between the
target (here, fundamental value) and the information that evokes affect determines both the use
of this affect-as-information heuristic in forming assessments of the target, and the efficacy of
this affect-as-information heuristic. To the extent that the affect-inducing information is less
related to the target, decision makers do not intentionally incorporate the affect-inducing
information into their target-related judgments. Instead, this affect-inducing information is
incorporated, via the application of the affect-as-information heuristic, to form target-related
judgments; such judgments are less likely to be on an informed basis, compared to one where
the affect-inducing information is more related to the target. In contrast, when the information
is related to the target, and decision makers are aware of the relevance of the information,
target-related judgments would default from reliance on heuristic processing to a more
systematic, deliberate process of directly forming assessments based on the target-relevant
information (Chaiken and Maheswaran 1994).5

whether it is related to the firm’s fundamental value.

Research Question: Is the effect of Explicit Assessment Symmetrical between Positive and
Negative CSR Performance?
Hypothesis 2 predicts that, when investors process immaterial CSR issues, prompting
investors to explicit assess CSR performance removes the positive effect of CSR performance
on investors’ fundamental value estimates. However, it does not specify whether the mitigating
mechanism (i.e., an explicit assessment of CSR performance, or “explicit assessment”) is
equally effective with positive versus negative CSR performance. 7
On the one hand, psychology research suggests that investors process positive versus
negative CSR information differently. There is evidence that negative affect is less likely to
lead to heuristic processing compared to positive affect (Bless et al. 1990; Bohner et al. 1992;

threat and thus leads to systematic processing aiming at solving the problematic situation. In
contrast, positive affect signals that the situation is unproblematic, and therefore, effortful
cognitive processing is less needed and the use of simple, heuristic strategies kicks in (Schwarz
et al. 1991). This account related to more careful systematic processing of negative information
is particularly pertinent in our setting for two related reasons. First, unlike most affect studies
in psychology where moods are either directly measured or induced in participants through a
task unrelated to their primary experimental task, 8 our study induces investors’

3. Experiment
Participants
One hundred and thirty-one MBA students from a major university in Korea
participated in our experiment.10 The participants have a mean working experience of 10 years.
On average, they have taken 2.8 accounting and 2.3 finance courses. All participants have used
financial statements to evaluate a firm’s performance at least once. Fifty-seven percent of the
participants have stock investment experience and seventy-seven percent plan to do so in the
next five years. Hence, our participants are reasonable proxies for informed investors as their
profiles on key attributes are comparable to those in Elliott et al. (2007). As in Elliott et al.
(2014), participants completed the experiment during a normally scheduled class, with the

Design

Material CSR in an Alternative Industry
As in Elliott et al. (2014), we chose the case of a firm in the apparel and accessory
industry. This choice potentially poses an external validity concern since the activities
identified as material (employees/supply chain and customers’ satisfaction) in the apparel and
accessories industry can be different from those in another industry. Therefore, we designed
another experiment with a hypothetical company set in the "Oil and Gas-Midstream industry,"
where the SASB points out that material activities are only related to environment and green
policies. Specifically, our material (immaterial) CSR performance condition involves
environmental impact and green policies (employees/supply chain and customers’ satisfaction),
and we employ an explicit assessment/positive CSR performance setting. Following our theory,
an explicit assessment of CSR performance will remove the CSR effect on fundamental value
assessments only in the immaterial condition (i.e., high CSR performance on employees/supply
chain and customers’ satisfaction) but not in the material condition (i.e., high CSR performance
on environmental impact and green policies). As a result, when investors are required to
explicitly assess the firm’s positive CSR performance, their fundamental value estimates will
be higher in the material condition compared to those in the immaterial condition, as investors
will realize that the latter does not directly link to the firm’s core business.

5. Conclusion
In this study, we conduct an experiment to investigate whether the relation between
CSR performance and investors’ fundamental value estimates is conditional on the materiality
and valence of the CSR indicators, and whether this effect is unintentional. Our results show
that when a firm’s overall CSR performance is driven by material CSR issues, investors’
fundamental value estimates are not affected by the presence or absence of an explicit
assessment of CSR performance, and this null effect holds for both positive and negative CSR
performance. When a firm’s overall CSR performance is driven by immaterial CSR issues,
however, investors’ fundamental value estimates are higher for positive CSR performance
when explicit assessment is absent than present, but are similar for negative CSR performance
whether the explicit assessment is present or absent. Our evidence suggests a heuristic approach
through which investors process immaterial and positive CSR indicators, and a systematic
approach through which investors process material or negative CSR indicators.
Our study extends Elliott et al. (2014) by showing that the relation between CSR
performance and investors’ estimates of fundamental value is affected by the materiality of
CSR issues, but only when CSR performance is positive. While Elliott et al. (2014) also
document an asymmetrical pattern between positive and negative CSR performance (see their
Table 2, Panel C), we show that this asymmetrical pattern exists only when the CSR issues are
immaterial but not when they are material. We also extend current literature on CSR by
providing evidence that the association between CSR performance and fundamental value
estimates operates through two distinct processes, depending on whether the CSR indicators
are material or immaterial. For immaterial CSR indicators, the process is an unintended effect
that is likely to operate through the use of a heuristic, and one that is mitigated in the presence
30
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3314723

of an explicit requirement to assess CSR performance, a mechanism designed and shown by
prior studies to eliminate the use of an affect-as-information heuristic. We show evidence of
this heuristic processing by demonstrating two findings: (a) the fundamental valuation
assessments are systematically lower in the presence (versus absence) of an explicit
requirement to evaluate positive CSR performance, and (b) this effect operates only in a theory-
consistent condition of positive CSR-performance and immaterial CSR issues. In contrast, for
material CSR issues, these two findings are not observed and hence, the process is unlikely to
be one involving the use of heuristic processing.
Our study has limitations. First, while we design the material and immaterial
conditions to comprise largely material and immaterial CSR indicators, respectively, firms may
actually have more mixed compositions. Further, our choice of material and immaterial
indicators was such that the link between the CSR activity and firm value was quite clearly
present or absent, and our participants were also able to affirm their materiality in post-
experiment tests. While this provides validity for our materiality construct, an issue that arises
is that, in practice, the materiality of some indicators is more ambiguous (e.g., energy saving
initiatives could be interpreted either as a green policy or as a strategy to reduce production
cost) and it will be hard to categorize them as either material or immaterial. Second, we used a
setting where the industry is experiencing moderate, not increasing, growth, and the company
has committed to cutting costs. These aspects can lead investors to react negatively towards
companies’ spending on immaterial CSR issues. Future research can examine whether our
results hold in a setting where the industry as a whole and/or the firm has not committed to cost
cutting measures. Third, we provide participants with a CSR performance report typically
available through independent third parties (i.e., similar to KLD), but investors can also rely
on a firm’s self-reported CSR information (e.g., websites, press releases, annual reports,
31
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3314723

standalone CSR reports and sustainability assurance). Compared to third-party CSR reports,
self-reported CSR information is less credible and investors may rely less on such information.
Consequently, it is conceivable that our documented effects relating to CSR materiality may be
muted given investors’ lower reliance in the case of self-reported CSR reports. Fourth, our
participants are from Korea, where investors’ characteristics and awareness of CSR activities
may be different from those in prior studies, notwithstanding evidence (in footnote 21) that our
participants have a similar understanding of fundamental value judgments to those in Elliott et
al. (2014). Finally, as in Elliott et al. (2014), our study assumes that participants hold a neutral
investing position. We do not investigate how investors’ directional preferences, i.e., investors
holding short versus long positions (Han and Tan 2010), can potentially influence the
processing of immaterial CSR metrics. Future research can examine whether immaterial CSR
performance associated with directional preferences also leads to heuristic processing, and
whether explicit assessments are sufficient to remove this unintended effect.
