You Have Been Forewarned! The Effects of Risk Management Disclosures
and Disclosure Tone on Investors’ Judgments
Abstract
We examine the effect of providing risk management disclosures alongside risk disclosures on
investors’ stock valuation judgments, and how risk disclosure tone influences this effect. In
practice, risk management disclosures are mandated in some settings but not others although
conceptually, disclosing both the risk and how it is managed offer investors the most holistic
understanding of firm risk. We find that without risk materialization, risk management
disclosures have a positive effect on judgments when the risk disclosure tone is positive. Also,
investors provide lower (higher) stock valuations when a positive versus negative tone is used in
the absence (presence) of risk management disclosures. However, with the negative news of risk
materialization, risk management disclosures and tone no longer have an effect on investors’
judgments. Our findings have implications for managers and regulators, as investors may
penalize firms that provide risk disclosures, even if these risks have been properly managed.
Keywords: risk disclosures, disclosure tone, disclosure regime, risk management plans

1. Introduction
Companies face various risks in their operations and take steps to manage them.
Conceptually, this gives rise to two aspects of a risk that firms can disclose— the risk faced
(hereafter, risk disclosures), and the steps taken to manage it (hereafter, risk management
disclosures). Theoretically, the disclosure of both aspects should allow investors to have the most
holistic understanding of firm risks. However, psychological theory (Creyer & Gurhan, 1997;
Hastie, 1984; Kelley, 1973; Reeder & Fulks, 1980) suggests that the absence or presence of risk
management disclosures could influence how investors respond to and process required risk
disclosures, leading to different disclosure outcomes. In practice, risk disclosure regimes differ in
terms of whether the second aspect, risk management disclosures, are mandated or provided
across settings. In this study, we focus on examining the effect of risk management disclosures
on investors’ judgments because of the debate on whether these disclosures should be provided
and their efficacy.
The SEC (2016, 2019b) has acknowledged that risks could be disclosed with or without
risk management disclosures. Both approaches have been mandated in different risk settings. For
example, the SEC’s disclosure standard for risk factor disclosures under Form 10-K (Regulation
S-K, Item 105) does not allow the simultaneous disclosure of risk management information.
While some firms discuss their risk management plans for specific risks through alternative
disclosure venues like the MD&A sections of their annual report or through conference calls, not
all firms do so. On the other hand, the SEC’s disclosure standard for market risks (Regulation S-
K, Item 305b) requires firms to also explain how disclosed risks are managed. This approach of

Given this difference in whether risk management disclosures are available, we examine

judgments. Disclosure tone is a salient feature given managerial discretion over risk disclosure
language, and the SEC does not regulate firms’ risk disclosure tone. Archival studies (Hanley &
Hoberg, 2010; Wang, 2020) find variances in risk disclosure tone (see Appendix A for risk
disclosure excerpts). Investors also recognize and respond to tone differences (Davis, Piger, &

setting, the availability of risk management disclosures could affect investors’ response to tone,
such that a positive/negative tone could be discounted in certain cases but not others.
We examine investors’ stock valuation judgments pre and post risk materialization. Pre
risk materialization settings are important to examine as risks do not always materialize, and
there may be a long time lag between risk disclosure and materialization if they do. Hence, the
pre risk materialization time periods likely represent the most frequent setting in practice. The

Investigating the effect of risk management disclosures on investors’ judgments is
theoretically and practically important. In terms of theoretical importance, attribution studies
from psychology (Creyer & Gurhan, 1997; Hastie, 1984; Kelley, 1973; Reeder & Fulks, 1980)
suggest that the presence of risk management disclosures could draw attention to the firm’s role
in contributing to, or failing to prevent, the negative outcomes of risk materialization. Thus, risk
management disclosures could influence how investors process and respond to risk information.
In terms of practical importance, there is currently a divergence in risk disclosure
approaches across risk settings and regimes. Investors and stock analysts rely on risk disclosures
in their investment and valuation decisions (Bradshaw, 2020; Niu, 2020; Palmer, 2022), but there
is both lack of consensus (SEC, 2019c) and empirical evidence on the effect of risk management
disclosures on their judgments. While managers could diligently manage risks, these risk
management plans may or may not be disclosed alongside risk disclosures due to disclosure
regulations. The absence or presence of risk management disclosures could influence investors’
stock valuation judgments, leading to different market outcomes even if management has
managed the risk to their best efforts and was forthcoming in their disclosures.
We conduct two experiments with participants recruited from Amazon Mechanical Turk
(MTurk) as proxies for nonprofessional investors. Our first experiment manipulates the absence
or presence of risk management disclosures and the tone of the corresponding risk disclosure
(i.e., positive or negative). To keep our tone manipulation consistent regardless of whether risk

management disclosures are provided, we only manipulate the tone of the corresponding risk
disclosure but not the tone of the risk management disclosures. We solicit participants’
judgments after they have read about the risk to examine investors’ judgments in a setting where
the risk does not materialize. Our second experiment manipulates the same factors as our first
experiment, but includes a press release which informs participants about the materialization of
the disclosed risk before soliciting their judgments.
We find that the effect of risk management disclosures on investors’ judgments depends
on the disclosure tone used and whether the risk has materialized. Without risk materialization,
we predict and find that the presence of risk management disclosures leads to higher stock
valuations than their absence. This effect is stronger when the corresponding risk disclosures is
made in a positive tone, which suggests that a positive disclosure tone is viewed more favorably
by investors when it is accompanied by specific steps taken to manage the risk but discounted
when it is not supported by tangible risk management actions. Comparatively, the effect of risk
management disclosures is more subdued with a negative tone, which suggests that a negative
tone does not alter how risk management disclosures are perceived.
When a risk materializes, we predict that the presence versus absence of risk management
disclosures should lead to lower stock valuations, with the effect stronger for a positive than
negative tone. However, we find evidence that risk management disclosures and disclosure tone
matter less given risk materialization. Taken together, these results suggest that while risk
management disclosures and disclosure tone have an effect on investors’ judgments before the
risk outcome is known, they matter less in a post risk materialization setting.
Our study contributes to theory and practice on risk disclosures. We extend theory on
how risk information could have different effects on investors’ judgments based on whether risk

management disclosures are present, and clarify the conditions (i.e., disclosure tone, whether the
risk materializes) under which risk management disclosures have a greater effect. Risk research
(Slovic, 1987; Slovic, Fischhoff, & Lichtenstein, 1980) suggests that having knowledge of risk
management plans can improve risk perceptions. In an accounting setting, Koonce, Lipe, and
McAnally (2008) find that a priori, information that a firm has hedged a financial risk could be
seen positively. Our study shows that in an operational risk disclosure setting, the disclosure of
risk management actions does not always result in positive investor reactions. Instead, the effect
of risk management disclosures depends on the disclosure tone used and whether the disclosed
risk has materialized.
Our theory on how risk management disclosures are processed could extend to other
accounting settings where risk and uncertainty disclosures are made. For example, auditors are
required to disclose critical audit matters (CAM) in the audit report and describe how their audit
procedures have addressed these uncertain or subjective matters. Similar to a risk disclosure
setting, these CAM disclosures are qualitative, and auditors have discretion over disclosure
language. At the same time, auditors’ disclosure of additional audit procedures could be seen as
audit risk management disclosures. These areas also have a higher chance of leading to negative
audit outcomes similar to a risk materialization, and investors’ judgments of auditor
responsibility could differ based on how the CAM was disclosed and/or addressed.
We contribute to practice by showing that investors’ stock valuation judgments differ
when risk management disclosures are present, and its effect depends on the disclosure tone and
risk materialization. Our results show that when risk management disclosures are absent as in the
current US risk factors setting, investors penalize firms that disclose unmaterialized risks in a
more positive versus negative tone. Upon risk materialization and in the absence of risk

perhaps disclose risks with a more cautionary tone when risk management disclosures are not

with their assessments of risk likelihood.
These results have implications for regulators who are considering mandating risk
management disclosures. If risks do not materialize, risk management disclosures increase
investors’ stock valuation judgments especially when disclosure tone is positive. If the regulatory
objective is to prompt more transparent risk disclosures, this finding could help regulators
achieve this by showing managers that there are potential upsides to disclosing well-managed
risks.

2. Theory and hypothesis development

Risk disclosures allow managers to communicate the diverse risks and threats facing their
firms. These forward-looking disclosures help investors better understand a firm’s operations
beyond the information conveyed in the financial statements. The SEC currently mandates
various forms of risk disclosure. For example, risk factor disclosures, which are “material factors
that make [an investment] speculative or risky” are mandated as Item 1A of a firm’s Form 10-K
filings (Regulation S-K, Item 105) while market risk disclosures are mandated as Item 7A
(Regulation S-K, Item 305). Besides these mandatory risk disclosures, managers may voluntarily
communicate risks to investors through the management discussion and analysis (MD&A)
section of the annual report or through earnings conference calls.
2.1. Risk management disclosures
Risk management disclosures describe the steps taken to manage a risk. There are
differences in whether risk management disclosures are mandated or provided across different
disclosure settings. For example, the SEC does not allow risk management disclosures as part of
Form 10-K risk factor disclosures because these disclosures discuss mitigating factors and are
not in line with the requirement to disclose “material factors that make [an investment]
speculative or risky” (Regulation S-K, Item 105). Thus, there is a concern that these disclosures
could dilute investors’ risk perceptions (SEC, 2016, p. 167). To enforce this in practice, SEC
staff have requested firms that provide risk management disclosures to remove them from their
risk factors section as they are viewed as mitigating information for a risk (SEC, 2020, 2021a).
Consistent with this, practitioners do not discuss risk management plans as part of their risk
factor disclosures, although they could provide these disclosures through other venues such as
conference calls or in the MD&A section (Gelfond, Wechsler, & Cohen, 2017; Sengupta & Xu,
2020).

We analyze a random sample of 20 firms’ Form 10-K risk factor disclosures, their
MD&A discussions, and corresponding conference call transcripts for the year 2019 to see if
they provide risk management disclosures for more specific risk factors through other venues.
We focus on more specific risk factors disclosed in the 10-K instead of more general risks like
the risk of competition, because management is more likely to discuss these general topics as
part of their conference calls or MD&As. Out of 20 firms, 10 firms discussed their risk
management plans for more specific risk factors in their MD&A and 13 firms discussed these
plans in their conference calls, sometimes as part of their response to analyst questions. This
shows that there is variation in terms of whether risk management disclosures are available to
investors in practice, which could influence investors’ response to these disclosed risks.
In contrast, the SEC mandates risk management disclosures for market risks (Regulation
S-K, Item 305b). Therefore, market risk disclosures must always be accompanied by a discussion
of how these risk exposures are managed and a general discussion of risk management strategies.
The SEC’s approach for market risks is also consistent with the approach adopted by regulators
in other jurisdictions. For example, German regulators mandate the disclosure of how each risk is
handled and the effectiveness of risk management systems used (ASCG, 2012; Dobler, 2005).
The European Union (Directive 2013/34/EU Articles 19a and 29a) mandates that public
companies disclose principal risks related to their operations and how these risks are managed.
The UK Corporate Governance Code (FRC, 2018, para. 28) requires the board of directors to
assess a company’s principal risks and describe the management of these risks in the annual
report on a “comply or explain” basis.
In their concept releases, the SEC (2016, 2019b) acknowledged the difference between
these two risk disclosure approaches and sought public comments on mandating risk

management disclosures in a risk factors setting. The requirement to provide these disclosures
has not been mandated to date (SEC, 2019c). Therefore, given the inconsistent regulations, risk
disclosures could either be made with or without risk management disclosures depending on the
disclosure setting and regime.
2.2. Risk disclosure tone
The qualitative and open-ended nature of risk disclosures provides managers with
considerable latitude when choosing their disclosure tone. In practice, managers could use a
positive tone to communicate less serious risks or temper investors’ reaction to risks.
Alternatively, managers who intend to forewarn investors about serious risks could use a more
negative tone. Archival studies (Hanley & Hoberg, 2010; Wang, 2020) find that differences in
risk disclosure tone lead to tangible market consequences. Wang (2020) finds that the risk
disclosure tone used in firms’ 10-K/Q filings affects their credit default swap premiums even
after controlling for disclosure volume. His findings suggest that risk disclosure tone plays an
incremental role in shaping debtholders’ risk perceptions. Hanley and Hoberg (2010) find that
the tone of the risk factors section in firms’ IPO prospectuses affects initial stock pricing, with a
more positive tone leading to increased pricing accuracy.
In general disclosure settings, managers vary their tone to achieve desired reporting
outcomes, and investors recognize and respond to variations in tone (Davis et al., 2012; Henry,
2008; Huang et al., 2013; Rogers, Van Buskirk, & Zechman, 2011; Tan et al., 2014). Huang,
Teoh, and Zhang (2013) find that managers vary disclosure tone to manage investors’
expectations and that an abnormal positive tone results in a more positive short-term market
response. Davis, Piper, and Sedor (2012) argue that managers use disclosure tone to signal
expectations about future firm performance. They find tone associated with market response and
9
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3804021

conclude that investors perceive tone as a credible signal of future performance. Rogers, Van
Buskirk, and Zechman (2011) find that managers’ use of optimistic language increases a firm’s
shareholder litigation risk. Taken together, these findings suggest that investors form
expectations about future firm performance and outcomes based on disclosure tone. In turn,
investors respond negatively when their expectations based on tone are violated, such as by
initiating lawsuits against the company (Rogers et al., 2011) or by revising their earlier
judgments (Huang et al., 2013).
2.3. Joint effect of risk management disclosures and tone (without risk materialization)
Risk management disclosures describe steps taken to manage a risk and prevent its
occurrence. Without risk materialization, we expect the presence of risk management disclosures
to positively influence investors’ risk perceptions and have a positive effect on investors’ stock
valuation judgments for two reasons. First, the implementation of risk management plans could
reduce the impact and/or likelihood of risk materialization. Thus, investors could react less
negatively to a disclosed risk upon knowing that it is managed by the firm (Slovic, 1987; Slovic
et al., 1980). Second, risk management disclosures communicate that management has
contemplated the risk and implemented ways to handle it, which suggests a higher degree of care
in managing the risk. Psychology research finds that a higher perceived degree of care results in
more favorable assessments of the decision maker (Reb & Connolly, 2010).
We predict that this positive effect of risk management disclosures on investors’ stock
valuation judgments will be greater when the corresponding risk disclosure is made in a positive
versus negative tone. Tone is used to signal managers’ expectations and investors are sensitive to
managers’ use of tone (Huang et al., 2013; Rogers et al., 2011). Tan et al. (2014) find that the use
of a positive tone in disclosures could backfire under certain circumstances, which suggests that

investors may react to a positive disclosure tone only if it is perceived as credible. Therefore, we
predict that investors are more likely to respond positively to managers’ use of a positive risk
disclosure tone when risk management disclosures are present, since the disclosure is supported
by tangible risk management steps. In particular, a positive tone alongside risk management
disclosures provides a credible incremental signal that management is better prepared and more
optimistic about the risk, which leads to more positive stock valuation judgments compared to a
case without risk management disclosures. In contrast, the use of a negative tone does not signal
that management is more optimistic about the risk, whether in the absence or presence of risk
management disclosures. This suggests that the positive effect of risk management disclosures
will be smaller if the corresponding risk disclosures are made in a negative tone.
This discussion suggests that without risk materialization, the positive effect of risk
management disclosures on investors’ judgments will be greater for a positive than negative
tone. Figure 1 depicts the possible patterns of results based on our predictions, and we state this
as our first hypothesis.
Hypothesis 1. Without risk materialization, the presence versus absence of risk management
disclosures will have a positive effect on investors’ stock valuations. This effect is more positive
when the corresponding risk disclosure is made with a positive versus negative tone.
2.4. Risk Materialization
Certain risks could still materialize despite firms’ risk management efforts. While risk
materialization happens relatively infrequently compared to the total volume of risks disclosed to
the markets, risk materialization are negative events that could lead to widespread market
consequences such as investor losses. The SEC (2019a, 2021b) has taken strong enforcement
actions against firms that did not disclose the materialization of previously disclosed risks, citing
the need for investor protection and consumer welfare. Similarly, US courts have ruled that the
11
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3804021

failure to disclose a risk’s materialization is a materially misleading omission (Posner, 2021).
These regulatory factors suggest that firms could be motivated to truthfully disclose the
materialization of risks and their consequences in a timely manner through press releases or as
part of their earnings guidance.
In the event of risk materialization, investors and regulators could refer to how a risk was
initially disclosed to see if managers provided adequate forewarning. The initial risk disclosure
could factor into the decision to commence legal action against the firm, and in the subsequent
assessment of damages. The materialization of risk doctrine is a well-established legal doctrine
through which plaintiffs seek compensation against a firm for inadequate risk disclosures (Booth,
2020). This approach involves scrutinizing a firm’s initial risk disclosures to assess the damages
resulting from those disclosures (Analysis Group, 2018). Thus, the way risks are initially
disclosed can play a role in shaping investors’ subsequent assessments, even if time has passed
between disclosure and materialization.
2.5. Joint effect of risk management disclosures and tone (with risk materialization)
In this section, we predict the effect of risk management disclosures given a
positive/negative disclosure tone if the disclosed risk materializes. Attribution studies find that
individuals spontaneously draw causal inferences when negative events occur (Hastie, 1984;
Kelley, 1973). At the same time, actions are more salient than words when drawing causal
inferences because actions draw attention to and highlight the actor’s causal role in contributing
to, or failing to prevent, the negative outcomes (Creyer & Gurhan, 1997; Reeder & Fulks, 1980).
This is consistent with findings in an audit setting, in which auditors who performed additional
procedures to investigate fraud were held more liable for undetected fraud than auditors who did
not (Reffett, 2010). The content of risk management disclosures which describe actions taken to
12

manage a risk could thus aid in the drawing of causal influences and blame assignment. Given
this, we expect investors to react more negatively in the presence versus absence of risk
management disclosures if a risk has materialized, since these disclosures highlight
management’s role in not preventing the negative outcomes of risk materialization.
This prediction is not without tension since an audit fraud risk setting could differ from a
risk disclosure setting. Specifically, risk management disclosures are made in advance of risk
materialization, and they could signal that managers took due care in managing the risk. If so,
investor reactions could be more positive in the presence versus absence of risk management
disclosures. In contrast, fraud detection procedures are not publicly disclosed in advance until a
fraud is discovered, and auditors are always expected to apply appropriate procedures to detect
fraud. Thus, auditors could be penalized more heavily for their failure to detect fraud.
We also predict that this negative effect of risk management disclosures on investment
judgments will be greater for a positive than negative risk disclosure tone. The use of a more
positive tone downplays the risk to a greater extent than a negative tone and could be perceived
as less appropriate in view of risk materialization. Hence, investors could react more negatively
to the news of risk materialization and penalize management more when a positive tone is used
in the presence of risk management disclosures. Comparatively, the presence versus absence of
risk management disclosures could have a smaller effect on decreasing investment judgments
when a negative tone is used. Investors are forewarned more strongly about the risk, and the
consequences of risk materialization are more expected with a negative tone. This suggests that
investors could react less negatively to the news of risk materialization and be less inclined to
penalize management in the presence of risk management disclosures with a negative tone.

3. Experiment 1
3.1. Design and procedure
We conducted a 2 (risk management disclosure – absent or present) by 2 (risk disclosure
tone – negative or positive) between-participants experiment to test our first hypothesis related to
how investors value the company’s stock prior to risk materialization. We examine investors’
stock valuation judgments, an investment related measure, because we are interested in the stock
market consequences of risk disclosure. More specifically, our research question examines
whether investors will penalize managers for how the same risk is disclosed. Therefore, as we
describe next, our research design holds the nature of the disclosed risk constant and only varies
the way in which the risk is disclosed (i.e., using a more positive or negative tone, and the
absence or presence of risk management disclosures).
Participants assume the role of an investor in Struttles Inc., a hypothetical sportswear
apparel company. We modeled our case material and risk disclosure after a sportswear company
listed on NASDAQ. Participants read background information and financial performance
information for Struttles, showing a steady increase in net revenue and stock price over the years.
Next, they read a disclosure about the risk of fabric supply disruption.

We manipulated risk disclosure tone by keeping the nature of the risk and risk
fundamentals the same but by using either positive or negative words to describe the outcome.
Participants in the positive (negative) disclosure tone condition were told: “Timely, uninterrupted

3.3. Manipulation of risk management disclosure
After reading a description of the risk and its potential consequences, participants in the
risk management disclosure present condition were shown an excerpt labeled “Risk Management
Plans” about how the company manages the risk: “To manage this risk of fabric supply
disruption, our company uses historical buying patterns to forecast fabric demand in advance.
This allows us to place fabric orders ahead of time and anticipate spikes in demand caused by
seasonality or marketing efforts. We also maintain a stockpile of reserve fabrics in the event of
an unexpected disruption.” Participants in the risk management disclosure absent condition did
not read this excerpt on how the risk is handled.
3.4. Participants
We recruited 173 participants from the Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) platform as
proxies for nonprofessional investors. Following accounting studies that rely on the same subject
pool (Brasel, Doxey, Grenier, & Reffett, 2016; Rennekamp, 2012; Rennekamp, Rupar, &
Seybert, 2014), the use of MTurk participants is appropriate for our task because we are
interested in the stock valuation judgments and risk perceptions of a general, nonprofessional

investing population. Participation was limited to subjects located in the United States with a
HIT approval rate of 97 percent or above. We pre-screened participants to ensure that they are
native English speakers, invest in the stock market personally and have taken at least one
accounting or finance course. Only those who fulfilled our pre-screening criteria were eligible to
participate in our experiment. To ensure data quality, we required participants to pass an
attention check and used the TurkPrime platform to block duplicate participants, previous
participants from our pilot rounds and verify participants’ location (Litman, Robinson, &
Abberbock, 2017).
One hundred and twenty-three participants (71.1 percent) are male. Participants have an
average stock investment experience of 9.6 years and average work experience of 17.8 years.
One hundred and fifty-five participants (89.6 percent) have at least a college degree or higher,
and they have taken an average of 2.53 (2.01) accounting (finance) courses. Participants spent an
average of 8.3 minutes on our task and were paid US$2 (for an equivalent hourly wage of
$14.50).
3.5. Dependent measures
After reading the company’s risk disclosures, participants responded to our main
dependent measure on how they would value the common stock of Struttles Inc. (on a 101-point
scale with 0 = Low value to 100 = High value). As a test of whether disclosure expectations were
met, we asked participants how satisfied they are with the company’s risk disclosures (0 = Not at
all satisfied to 100 = Completely satisfied). To test whether a positive tone increases perceptions
of risk preparedness in the presence of risk management disclosures, we asked participants how
well-prepared Struttles is against a fabric supply disruption (0 = Poorly prepared to 100 = Well-
prepared).

The presence of a tone effect when risk management disclosures are made but the lack of
a tone effect when risk management disclosures are absent may be explained through the
interplay of two forces: the salience of negative news arising from risk materialization, and the
causal inferences made possible by risk management disclosures. Specifically, news of the risk
materialization is an overwhelmingly salient and negative signal that can draw investor attention.
At the same time, individuals have a proclivity to make causal inferences when given bad news
(Hastie, 1984; Kelley, 1973)—risk management disclosures highlight the firm’s role in failing to
prevent the negative outcomes of risk materialization and aid in the drawing of these causal
inferences (Creyer & Gurhan, 1997; Reeder & Fulks, 1980). In the absence of risk management
disclosures, only the salience of the negative news is present, and it draws investor attention
away from the risk disclosure and reduces the effect of tone on investors’ judgments. In the
presence of risk management disclosures, attention is moved somewhat to the risk disclosure and
actions management claimed they made, allowing the effect of tone to take place.

5. Conclusion
We conduct two experiments to examine how risk management disclosures affect
investors’ stock valuation judgments, and whether this effect is influenced by the risk disclosure
tone used. We examine investors’ judgments in a pre and post risk materialization setting. Our
research question is motivated by theoretical differences in how a risk can be communicated—
with or without the corresponding risk management disclosures and with possible differences in
the disclosure tone used.
Psychology theory (Creyer & Gurhan, 1997; Hastie, 1984; Kelley, 1973; Reeder & Fulks,
1980) suggests that the presence of risk management disclosures could alter how investors
process and respond to risks. In practice, risk settings and regimes differ in terms of whether risk
management disclosures are currently provided or mandated. For example, the SEC does not
allow risk management disclosures as part of firms’ Form 10-K risk factor disclosures but
mandates these risk management disclosures for market risks. Similarly, risk management
disclosures are mandated in other regimes (e.g., Germany, UK, and the EU). There is also
variation in whether firms discuss their risk management plans as part of their conference calls or
in the MD&A section of their annual reports, and even firms that disclose these plans do not
uniformly do so for all their disclosed risks. Disclosure tone is another salient feature in the risk
disclosure setting given that the SEC does not regulate firms’ use of tone. Managers can
communicate firm risks in a more positive or negative tone (Hanley & Hoberg, 2010; Wang,
2020), and tone could influence how risk management disclosures are perceived.
This research question is important because psychology research suggests that investors’
response to risk disclosures could differ based on how the risk was initially disclosed and
whether it has materialized, leading to different disclosure consequences. Having knowledge
23
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3804021

about how investors respond to risk management disclosures with and without risk
materialization could help managers make more informed disclosure decisions. We conduct two
experiments using participants recruited from the MTurk platform as proxies for nonprofessional
investors. In the first experiment involving a setting without risk materialization, we find that the
presence versus absence of risk management disclosures leads to higher stock valuations, with a
stronger effect for a positive versus negative disclosure tone. Also, when risk management
disclosures are absent (present), investors provide lower (higher) stock valuation judgments in
response to a disclosed risk when a positive (negative) risk disclosure tone is used. In the second
experiment involving a setting where the disclosed risk has materialized, we find that risk
management disclosures and disclosure tone matter less once investors are aware of the risk
materialization.
Our results have important implications for theory and practice. First, our theory on how
risk management disclosures are processed could extend to other accounting settings where risk
and uncertainty disclosures are made, and where disclosed risks sometimes materialize (e.g., the
disclosure of CAMs in the audit report).
Second, our findings have implications for practice. Investors and managers likely prefer
the simultaneous disclosure of risk management plans alongside risk disclosures. These plans
allow for a more holistic understanding of firm risks and could temper investors’ negative risk
perceptions. At the same time, managers are likely to warn about risks with a more negative
tone. Our findings show that investors’ perception of these risk disclosures differ based on
whether risk management disclosures are also made and whether the risk has materialized. The
use of a negative tone to warn about risks in a setting where risk management plans are also
disclosed leads to less favorable investor judgments than a positive tone when the risk has not
24
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3804021

materialized. On the other hand, the use of a positive risk disclosure tone to assure investors
about firm risks backfires if the risk was disclosed with risk management plans, with investors
penalizing the firm more upon risk materialization. This poses a concern since a firm could have
been forthcoming in their risk disclosures and could also have managed the risk to the best of
their abilities prior to its occurrence. These results suggest that managers should adjust their
disclosure tone based on whether risk management disclosures are provided and their
assessments of risk materialization likelihood to avoid being penalized by the market for risk
disclosures.
Our study is subject to several limitations which also provide opportunities for future
research. First, we examine the lay risk judgments of nonprofessional investors. We believe this
segment of the investing population is important given that they form the majority of investors
on the market and may not have the professional judgment needed to accurately assess risks. Our
results may thus be different for other groups of investors (e.g., professional investors, analysts
or risk management professionals). Second, we based our scenario on the disclosure and
materialization of a single risk factor which had relatively severe consequences. Third, we only
manipulate the tone of the disclosed risk and hold the tone of our risk management disclosure
constant across conditions. We made this design choice to keep our risk disclosure tone
manipulation consistent regardless of whether risk management disclosures are provided. In
practice, either the risk disclosure or risk management disclosure, or both, could vary in tone.
Fourth, the effect of risk disclosures on stock valuation judgments could likely happen through
multiple paths beyond those specified in our theory and examined in our experiments. Future
research may find it fruitful to explore how investors respond to the disclosure of multiple risk

disclosure features that could also have an effect on investors’ stock valuation judgments.
