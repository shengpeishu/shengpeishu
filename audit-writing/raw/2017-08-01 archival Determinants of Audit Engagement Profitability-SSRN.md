Determinants of Audit Engagement Profitability
ABSTRACT
We examine determinants of audit engagement profitability using proprietary data from the
national office of a Big 4 public accounting firm in Canada. We verify through interviews with
senior audit partners that the audit realization rate is the primary measure used to assess
engagement level profitability, and use the former to proxy for engagement profitability in our
archival analysis. We find that engagement profitability is positively associated with the firm’s
assignment of lead senior audit managers as identified by the national office, and its delivery of
intangible client service dimensions such as communication, customization, and responsiveness
(obtained from satisfaction surveys of client management and audit committee chairs). We find
that audit quality, measured by discretionary accruals and audit adjustments, is not sacrificed to
attain higher engagement profitability. We discuss implications for regulation and competition
in audit markets.
Keywords: audit engagement realization rates, auditor expertise, client service delivery, client
satisfaction

Determinants of Audit Engagement Profitability
I. INTRODUCTION
In this study, we employ a proprietary dataset obtained from the national office of a Big 4
public accounting firm in Canada to investigate two determinants of the profitability of public
accounting firms’ audit client engagements: auditor expertise and intangible client service
dimensions (e.g., communication, responsiveness, and service customization). Overly high or
low audit engagement profitability is a concern of regulators (PCAOB 2008) and professional
bodies (National Association of State Boards of Accountancy 2010).1
The linkage between profitability and the conduct of audits has led to regulatory guidance
on voluntary disclosure of public accounting firm profitability in the United Kingdom
(Consultative Committee of Accountancy Bodies 2009). If the profitability of an audit
engagement is low, auditors may have incentives to “cut corners” and perform a lower quality
audit, which is an issue raised by the financial press (Weil 2004) and regulatory bodies (e.g.,
PCAOB 2008 and Goelzer 2003]). Conversely, it is problematic if a highly profitable audit
engagement comes from reduction of audit hours, or from auditors’ acquiescence to client’s
accounting preferences (Kadous, Kennedy, and Peecher 2003). The literature on an auditor’s
economic bonding to the client also focuses on audit engagement profitability as its central
construct (DeAngelo 1981; Dye 1991). Not surprisingly, audit partners who we interviewed for
this study indicated that the firms monitor an audit engagement’s profitability, and use it to
assess a partner’s performance. Given the various consequences associated with audit
engagement profitability, it is important to identify its antecedents. If these antecedents of audit

The two determinants of audit engagement profitability that we examine represent ways
that a public accounting firm can earn higher profits by differentiating its product from its
competitors (Barney 1991; Caves and Porter 1977; Porter 1980; Klein and Leffler 1981). The
first factor we examine is the expertise of lead personnel assigned to the audit engagement, a
factor that is generally associated with higher audit quality (Knechel, Krishnan, Pevzner,
Shefchik, and Velury 2013). As firms that deliver professional services, public accounting firms
make investments in human capital to develop expertise, generate competitive advantage, and
distinguish themselves from competitors (Barney 1991). Clients’ demand for experts to deliver
high quality audits can contribute to higher audit engagement profitability. Prior research
identifies accounting firm industry specialization as a key determinant of audit fees, suggesting
that clients pay a price premium for industry expertise (Simunic 1980; O’ Keefe, King, and
Gaver 1994b; Dopuch, Gupta, Simunic, and Stein 2003; Ferguson, Francis, and Stokes 2003;
Fung, Gul, and Krishnan 2012). However, whether higher fees from employing expert resources
translate to higher profitability is unclear, because such expert resources are costly (Sharp and
Dawes 2001) and experts may actually require better quality evidence that entails more costly
audits.
The second factor we examine is intangible client service delivery, which can increase or
decrease audit quality, and is a very much under-researched construct in the audit literature. It is
another way a public accounting firm can achieve higher engagement profitability by
differentiating itself from its competitors (Barney 1991; Porter 1980; Klein and Leffler 1981).

Intangible service and corporate culture/coordination practices are difficult for competitors to

competitive advantage (Barney 1991; Porter 1980). In an audit setting, intangible client service
delivery refers to uncontroversial audit execution activities, such as effective and timely
communication with clients, providing timely and helpful responses to the clients’ questions, and
customization of the firm’s services to the client’s specific needs (Fiolleau, Hoang, Jamal, and
Sunder 2013; Maister 1993).2 These intangible client service activities are directly experienced
by senior management executives, audit committee members, and other client personnel who
determine audit fee levels. Clients who are pleased with the services provided by the public
accounting firm on these distinctive dimensions are more likely to pay an audit fee premium; in
turn, better communication, coordination and execution can also generate cost efficiency. This
can translate to higher audit engagement profitability to the extent that the incremental costs of
providing these service dimensions are lower than the incremental fees collected. In addition,
while the audit literature has primarily thought of demand for audit as being driven by external
users of financial statements who want independence and expertise, there is also an internal
demand from client managers for the audit to manage moral hazard within the organization
(Jensen and Meckling 1976). Auditors’ ability to meet client service demands can be a proxy for
how well the auditor assists management in managing internal moral hazard, though paying
higher fees for client service could also be a source of economic bonding that impairs auditor
independence.

To examine our research questions, we analyze post-SOX proprietary data on sixty audit
clients from a Big 4 public accounting firm in Canada. The engagement realization rates
provided by the accounting firm form our proxy measure for audit engagement profitability. The
participating Big 4 firm provided two actual realization rates: an overall rate for the engagement
(which encompasses all professional services) and a stand-alone rate for the audit service only.
An audit engagement realization rate is a type of standard full cost (variable and fixed) plus
profit recovery measure, where fees actually collected are compared to standard fees for hours
worked on the engagement (see Appendix A for measurement details). The participating firm’s
realization rates are calculated as follows. A standard fee is determined by using a full-cost-plus-
profit charge-out rate for each grade of labor multiplied by the actual number of hours worked on
the engagement. The actual realization rate equals fees collected for the engagement divided by
this standard fee. A higher realization rate implies higher profitability, given that the standard
charge-out rate incorporates a profit element.3
While this realization rate is not a pure profitability measure per se as it is indirect and
imperfect, it includes both a revenue dimension (actual fees collected) and a cost dimension
(incorporated within standard fees budgeted). Since the realization rate is an indicator of whether
the actual fees cover the costs of producing the audit (Hackenbrack and Hogan 2005), it is
arguably a better proxy for profitability (and economic bonding between the accounting firm and
the client) than standard measures used in the audit literature, such as audit fees or abnormal

Controlling for client characteristics, we find that audit engagement profitability is driven
by technical expertise of the lead audit senior manager but not that of the lead partner. Consistent
with our expectations, the client’s satisfaction assessments of intangible service dimensions—the
auditor’s communication, responsiveness, and customization—are important explanatory
variables for audit engagement realization rates. This set of variables is also significant for the
profitability of the overall client engagement. Audit quality measures are not associated with
either audit or overall realization rates, suggesting that auditors do not sacrifice audit quality in
the bid to boost engagement profitability. In additional analyses, we find that clients’ overall
satisfaction with the firm is driven by perceptions of communication, technical expertise,
responsiveness, and customization. However, clients that are assigned the public accounting
firm’s top experts are not more satisfied compared to clients that are not assigned top experts.
These findings indicate that the determinants of client satisfaction may not be based on technical
accounting expertise or resolution of technical accounting issues, as client personnel (even
though they are CFOs or audit committee members) may have difficulty judging the expertise
level of the audit team.
Our study adds to our understanding of the role of expertise and intangible service
differentiation in determining engagement profitability. To our knowledge, our study is the first
to examine this issue. Our proprietary dataset offers unique insights to this issue, and brings
together arguably better and rarely accessible proxies for the three key constructs in our study.
Specifically, we proxy engagement profitability using realization rates; this measure has both
internal and external validity in that public accounting firms confirm they employ it in their

II. BACKGROUND AND HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT
Research in strategy and industry organization suggests that a firm wishing to earn
abnormal profits via a differentiation strategy must credibly differentiate its products from those
of its competitors (Barney 1991; Porter 1980). Porter (1980) identifies a series of forces that can
help an industry to become more profitable than other industries due to various barriers to entry
(e.g., patents) and other attributes (e.g., large investments in brands) that limit the effect of
competition. Barney (1991) extends these insights to a company level, where individual companies
develop differentiation in a manner that cannot be easily replicated by competitors.
Barney (1991) develops a resource-based model of a firm whereby three types of capital
can help create competitive advantage for a company. The first relates to the physical capital of

the firm (Williamson 1975). This consists of technology, plant and equipment, geographical
location, or access to raw materials. Since many types of physical resources can be purchased in
markets, there must be something special about a firm’s physical resources (e.g., first mover access
to a resource that other firms cannot get) to create competitive advantage. Second, human capital
can create competitive advantage (Becker 1964). This consists of intelligence, experience, training,
judgment, relationships, and insight of people who work for a firm. Many of these attributes are
intangible and difficult for outsiders to imitate, so they can create enduring competitive advantage.
Third, organizational capital and practices can create competitive advantages (Tomer 1987). These
consist of firm culture, reporting and information sharing practices, planning, control and
coordination systems, as well as informal relationships among its workers. Organizational capital
is embodied either in organizational relationships, particular members of organizations, the
organization’s repositories of information, or some combination of the above. Again, many of
these attributes are intangible, difficult for outsiders to observe, and thus difficult for competitors
to understand and imitate.
For public accounting firms, it is difficult to identify any physical resources that can create
competitive advantage, but it is very plausible that differentiation and, therefore, higher
profitability can be achieved through two avenues: (a) investments in human capital, such as the
provision of specialized technical expertise; and (b) investments in organizational capital, such as
culture and coordination practices that affect the client experience (Barney 1991; Klein and Leffler
1981). These two avenues are potential drivers of profitability that are helpful (or have no effect)
in improving audit quality. For instance, providing specialized technical knowledge has been
associated with better audit quality (Reichelt and Wang 2010), and an improved client experience

likely leads to a more cooperative client who is more amenable to agreeing with the auditor on
difficult accounting choices/audit adjustments (Hatfield, Agoglia and Sanchez 2008).
We also investigate a possible profitability driver that can have a deleterious effect on audit
quality. Specifically, it is possible that auditors can achieve high profitability by pandering to the
client’s preferences by permitting aggressive accounting choices or waiving audit adjustments. We
discuss the effects of each of these three drivers below.
Data on audit realization rates has been reported in a few studies (Simunic and Stein 1996;
Dopuch et al. 2003; Bedard and Johnstone 2004; Hackenbrack and Hogan 2005; Schelleman and
Knechel 2010). However, the relation between expertise or other determinants and profitability is
not the primary focus of these studies; rather, the primary focus of these studies is to examine audit
production efficiencies and/or how auditors respond to increased earnings management risk. The
key findings are that production efficiency varies widely across audit engagements and that
auditors do not simply charge higher audit fees to riskier clients and generate greater short-term
profits. Instead, auditors respond to risk by increasing their effort (Schelleman and Knechel 2010),
being more selective in accepting/ retaining clients (Bedard and Johnstone 2004), or modifying
their audit report (Schelleman and Knechel 2010). We differ from these studies in that, rather than
focusing on audit effort and costs, we investigate how realization rates serve as a factor on which
public accounting firms compete to achieve profits by making deliberate choices about the
determinants of profitability.
Provision of Specialized Technical Expertise
In the context of auditing, a knowledge-intensive service, differentiation can be attained
through an aspect of human capital, namely, technical industry expertise. A public accounting
firm’s technical expertise is a major determinant that enables the firm to charge a fee premium.

Evidence of an expertise price premium has been documented in studies on auditor industry
specialization and market leadership (O’ Keefe et al. 1994b; Ferguson et al. 2003; Francis et al.
2005; Fung et al. 2012).4 Underlying the price premium is the notion that public accounting firms
make investments in intellectual capital and industry expertise, and in doing so, they are able to
earn higher audit fees (O’ Keefe, Simunic, and Stein 1994a; Simunic and Stein 1996). An argument
can also be made that public accounting firms will not continue to invest in expertise, nor continue
efforts to signal expertise of top personnel, if there are no economic benefits to be reaped.
However, there is tension in this argument. Investments in intellectual capital and allocation of
expert resources are costly for public accounting firms; thus, it is an open question whether
technical expertise does lead to greater engagement profitability (Sharp and Dawes 2001).5
Indirect evidence can be found in Schelleman and Knechel (2010), who show that the response to
higher risk leads to higher audit fees and auditor effort, but not higher audit realization rates. We
note that other than technical expertise, the audit partner and the senior manager who possess tacit
managerial knowledge (Tan and Libby 1997) can also generate economic returns through superior
skills in delivering valuable client service intangibles (discussed in the next section), negotiating
fee premiums and/or by controlling the costs of performing the audit.
In summary, the proxies for engagement profitability and auditor expertise in prior research
are based on externally observable data on audit fees and industry leadership. Our study uses client
engagement realization rates as a relatively more direct measure of profitability, and internally
recognized specialization as a relatively more direct measure of individual auditors’ expertise. We

Intangible Client Service Delivery
The second product differentiation approach relates to the use of organizational capital
such as culture and coordination practices to differentiate the auditor’s product from its
competitors. This organizational mechanism includes client-specific investments that provide
direct service value to customers and enhance customer satisfaction. Examples of such
investments include training staff to be responsive to client needs, understanding internal agency
issues specific to the client organization, assigning relationship partners to clients, and
maintaining continuity of staff. These investments in service delivery are privately experienced
and observed by clients while being difficult for competitors to observe and imitate. Delivering
superior audit service with these features represents a competitive strategy that could raise
switching costs for clients and defend the public accounting firm against competitors’ products
(Porter 1980). Attributes that are observable only to the client, such as intangible service delivery
(communication, customization, and responsiveness), contribute to the public accounting firm’s
profits to the extent that the client values these attributes. Barney’s (1991) resource-based model
posits that the prospect of profits for the public accounting firm will motivate the firm to provide
high quality client service.
There has been much research on the relationship between audit quality and industry
expertise, office size, or various levels and sources of fees derived from audit, tax, and other non-
audit services). However, less attention has been paid to client characteristics (Lawrence,

Minutti-Meza, and Zhang 2011), labor costs (Bell, Doogar, and Solomon 2008), or service
quality attributes that lead to client satisfaction and audit fee premiums (see Behn, Carcello,
Hermanson, and Hermanson 1999 as a notable exception). Professional service firm consultants
who offer advice on client relationship management (Maister 1997) and practitioner-oriented
books and articles (e.g., Dunn and Baker 2003) emphasize the importance of client service
delivery (e.g., communication, responsiveness, and making the client feel important) and
industry expertise as factors leading to higher profits. Likewise, a recent field study on how a
client chooses an auditor (Fiolleau et al. 2013) indicates that differentiation among Big 4 firms
often occurs through non-audit specialty knowledge (especially tax) and service attributes, such
as perceived responsiveness, commitment to the client management team, and treating the client
as a high priority engagement. These practitioner resources and field study findings are
consistent with Porter’s (1980) and Barney’s (1981) competitive strategy of differentiation, in
that they advocate the qualities that can be observed and valued by customers but cannot be
easily copied by competitors.
In the marketing literature, Anderson, Fornell, and Lehmann (1994) report that higher
satisfaction increases customer loyalty and willingness to pay premiums to the firm, leading to a
higher return on investment for the firm. This suggests that, to the extent that provision of quality
client service delivery leads to higher client satisfaction, higher engagement profitability will
result. Again, there is tension in this argument. Specifically, if client personnel hire auditors
mainly for the benefit of external stakeholders, they may not value any of the customer service
dimensions offered by the auditor. The auditor may be hired only to meet the demands of
external agents, and the client personnel (and external stakeholders) may have difficulty in
assessing the quality of service provided by the auditor. In this case, the auditor is providing a

credence good (Knechel et al. 2013), and may not earn any excess returns by investing in client

In summary, we posit that client service delivery is an important determinant of audit
client profitability. This is because the auditor helps management deal with internal agency
conflicts, and better coordination with management achieves efficiencies in carrying out the
audit. Public accounting firms’ desire to satisfy clients and deliver on service dimensions to
strengthen client relationships is a competitive strategy that motivates public accounting firms to
deliver client service quality (Maister 1997; Behn et al. 1999). Hence, while there are arguments
against greater engagement profitability associated with client service delivery, we expect that,
as in other customer service settings, auditors are in a stronger position to extract economic rents
from the clients that are pleased with their work on important service dimensions. This is
particularly true because client service expectations are set and directly experienced by the senior
client executives and the audit committee members who determine audit fee levels. We state our
hypothesis as follows:
H2: Audit engagement profitability is positively associated with client service delivery.
Audit Quality Concessions
In developing H2, part of the premise is that auditors are better able to extract economic
rents from clients that are pleased with the auditors’ services. While the factors we discussed earlier
are benign or potentially helpful to audit quality, one possible audit-quality decreasing way for
auditors to please the clients is to waive income-decreasing audit adjustments that are initially
proposed by auditors. Prior research indicates that auditor-client negotiations over disagreements
related to financial reporting decisions and audit adjustments are common (Gibbins, Salterio, and

Webb 2001), and that auditors do offer concessions on proposed audit adjustments (Nelson, Elliott,
and Tarpley 2002). Research shows that these concessions on income-decreasing audit
adjustments positively influence client’s satisfaction and intention to continue a relationship with
the auditor (Tan and Trotman 2010), and have multi-period consequences (Cheng et al. 2016).
Accordingly, it is possible that in a bid to increase the client’s satisfaction and therefore an
engagement’s profitability, auditors could resort to offering more concessions to the client by
waiving proposed audit adjustments or permitting aggressive accounting choices that compromise
audit quality. However, a disincentive against these actions is the possibility of severe reputation
and financial costs to the auditor associated with restatements and potential audit failure (Skinner
and Srinivasan 2012). We state the following research question:
RQ: Is audit engagement profitability associated with lower audit quality?
III. METHOD
Audit Partner Interviews about Realization Rates
To better understand the role of realization rates in public accounting firms, we
conducted interviews with senior partners of nine public accounting firms (all four Big 4 firms
and five other firms). Five partners were the managing partners of a local office, one was head of
audit in the local office, one was a head office quality control partner, and two were senior audit
partners referred to us by the local office managing partner; all partners were located in Canadian
offices. We asked them four primary questions: (a) What are their firm’s primary performance
measures at the engagement level? (b) What are other performance measures at the engagement
level? (c) What are the performance measures at the partner level? (d) What makes a client a
“priority” client? Appendix B provides a summary of the interviewee’s responses.

We proxy the construct, audit engagement profitability, using actual client realization

Engagement Characteristics
We control for the following engagement characteristics: the mix of audit and non-audit
services (NASPERCENT); whether the engagement takes place during busy season (BUSY);
whether the client is new to the public accounting firm (NEW); and auditor effort in terms of
total audit hours for all personnel at all ranks (HOURS). These variables have been associated
with audit efficiency in prior literature (O’Keefe et al. 1994b; Simunic and Stein 1996; Dopuch
et al. 2003; Hay et al. 2006; Causholli et al. 2010).
NASPERCENT is the percentage of total engagement fees that are attributable to non-
audit services (hereafter “NAS”). Dopuch et al. (2003) report higher realization rates for clients
with a larger proportion of NAS fees. Client engagements with high NAS fees may be more
profitable overall if NAS contribute higher margins (Simunic 1980) and/or there is knowledge
spillover from providing NAS that enhance audit efficiency (Dopuch et al. 2003). The previously
reported findings are based on data prior to auditor independence reforms that limited public
accounting firms’ provision of NAS to audit clients; these reforms may have altered the
relationship between the service mix and profitability. In our sample, on average, 30 percent of
engagement fees are from NAS (median = 28 percent).
BUSY is coded one if the audit is conducted during busy season (i.e., the client has a
December 31 fiscal year end). The public accounting firm may accept lower margins or fees
outside of busy season to utilize audit personnel during idle times, and prior research reports

V. CONCLUSION
This study investigates the variables that create engagement profitability for public
accounting firms. We provide evidence that individual auditors’ expertise and intangible service
dimensions are positively associated with engagement profitability (proxied using realization
rates), and that greater engagement profitability is not associated with lower audit quality. While
prior research has focused only on audit attributes that may be observable to external users of
audit reports (such as public accounting firm size and industry expertise), we contribute evidence
that audit clients also value intangible service attributes and expert personnel assignment that
they experience privately in the auditor-client relationship.

Our examination of proprietary data on engagement realization rates takes into account
audit production costs and clients’ willingness to pay a fee premium. While realization rates are
not a pure measure of profitability, they do incorporate both revenue and cost data, thus
providing a better but not perfect measure of profitability than fee data. On the tangible
dimensions of audit quality that are externally observable (e.g., client size), we find a significant
relationship with both fees and realization rates, though some variables (e.g., busy season
engagement) that have a significant effect on realization rates do not have a significant effect on
fees. For senior manager expertise and the intangible service delivery factors of customization
and responsiveness, we find a significant relationship with realization rates, but not fees.
Our study of client satisfaction survey responses from managers and audit committee
members allows us to test of what audit clients perceive they like about their public accounting
firm, measured by audit quality dimensions that clients privately observe and experience. We
find that perceptions of communication, technical expertise, responsiveness, and customization
are the service delivery factors that are positively associated with client satisfaction. Importantly,
the service delivery factors that generate engagement profitability differ somewhat from those
that contribute to client satisfaction. While perceived communication, customization, and
responsiveness are positively associated with audit and overall realization rates, perceived
technical expertise is not. These findings imply that when clients perceive greater technical
expertise, they are more satisfied, but perceived technical expertise does not translate into greater
profitability for the engagement.

expertise (Fiolleau et al. 2013).

relationships. Regulators have been concerned about the link between audit engagement
profitability and the conduct of the audit and its associated quality. Our results show that auditor
expertise (a feature that can enhance audit quality) and client service delivery (a feature that can
enhance or have no effect on audit quality) influence audit engagement profitability. On the latter
feature, to address independence concerns, regulators have sought to weaken auditor-client
bonds (e.g., the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002), and the public accounting firms’ assignment of
clients to “priority” status raises questions about auditor independence. We find that

examine a sample of clients from one Big 4 public accounting firm with a formal service quality
program that includes routine survey feedback and dedicated head office personnel. The data are
also from surveys designed and administered by the public accounting firm, and thus we had no
control over the content or the administration of the survey. For instance, in our study, the client
satisfaction survey relates to overall satisfaction for audit and non-audit services, and we are
unable to partition out the satisfaction levels related to each. Further, the satisfaction survey was
conducted between 2007-2010, which corresponded to a less favorable economic period wherein
audit firms may face pressure from clients to reduce audit fees as a result of the economic
conditions, and not so much because of dissatisfaction with service quality. We cannot address
the impact of economic conditions with data from only one time-period, although we note that
weak economic conditions would also bias against our ability to detect any association between
service quality and realization rates. In future studies, researchers can consider collecting this

APPENDIX A
Relationship between Realization Rate and Audit Fee
Realization Rate per Engagement = Fees Collected ÷ Standard Rate, where:
Fees Collected = total fees collected for the client engagement, excluding incidental fees that are
billed separately (e.g. PCAOB fees, incidentals, and travel expenses)
Standard Rate = sum of (total number of hours worked on the engagement by each grade of
labor x standard charge-out rate) for all engagement personnel (by grade of
labor)
Note that this measure reflects the actual realization rate, rather than planned realization rate for
the engagement, which would be calculated based on planned number of hours worked for
planned personnel. The planned rate is used to develop a quote to provide the client and agree
upon a fixed engagement fee prior to the engagement. The standard charge-out rate is based on
cost-plus-profit for each rank of personnel for each office.
We propose that this realization rate measure is a proxy for the profitability of each client
engagement, as it takes into account the fee and the cost of the engagement. While this measure
captures a profit component for efficiency with which the work is performed and auditors’
bargaining power in negotiating and collecting finalized fees from the client, it can also be
impacted by personnel assignment choices, within-rank rate differences and auditors’ forecasting
ability, as illustrated in the scenarios below (assuming the audit fee is fixed).
