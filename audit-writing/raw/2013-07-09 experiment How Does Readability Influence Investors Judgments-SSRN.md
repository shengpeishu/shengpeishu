How Does Readability Influence Investors’ Judgments?
Consistency of Benchmark Performance Matters
ABSTRACT: We conduct an experiment to investigate how readability (high vs. low)
and benchmark performance consistency (consistent vs. inconsistent) influence investors’
judgments. Using prior management guidance and year-ago quarter performance as two
benchmarks against which to assess actual earnings performance, we manipulate whether
performance evaluations relative to these two benchmarks are consistent with each other.
Our findings show that readability has a magnified impact on investors’ performance
judgments when benchmark performance is inconsistent than when it is consistent. We
find that high readability improves investors’ understanding of the firm’s positive
(negative) current period performance relative to year-ago quarter performance, and this
leads to higher (or lower) performance judgment. We also find that readability influences
disclosure credibility and performance judgment through processing fluency only in the
absence of conflicting benchmark performance, but not in its presence. These findings
provide insights on the mechanisms (i.e., understanding and processing fluency) through
which readability influences investors’ judgments, and the conditions under which each
operates.
Keywords: readability; benchmark performance consistency; trend performance;
understanding; processing fluency; performance judgment

I. INTRODUCTION
The conceptual framework for financial reporting issued by Financial Accounting
Standards Board (FASB) states that information should be classified, characterized, and
presented clearly and concisely to make it understandable (SFAC No. 8).1 In response to
the increasing emphasis on providing clear and readable financial disclosures, the SEC
requires companies to follow plain English writing principles in documenting their
financial disclosures, on the assumption that improved readability facilitates investors’
understanding and helps investors make more informative decisions (SEC 1998).
In contrast, some managers presumably believe that there are benefits (from their
perspective) to provide low readability disclosures. Consistent with this view, Li (2008)
documents that managers tend to use low readability disclosures to obfuscate poor
earnings performance. In fact, concerns about readability in management disclosures
likely involve those where there are inconsistencies in the message conveyed. Prior
studies suggest that managers discuss alternative financial measures and non-financial
measures in their disclosures, and inconsistency among different measures is common
(Francis et al. 2003; Brazel et al. 2009). In the presence of conflicting measures,
managers who strategically provide selective low readability disclosures likely do so
when there are some measures in the disclosure that reflect negatively on the
firm/management, but not do so for other measures in the disclosure that have positive or
neutral implications.

While management disclosures vary in terms of whether the communicated message

message attribute. Extant research documents that high readability magnifies investors’
reactions to the information contained in a disclosure (e.g., You and Zhang 2009; Miller

extended to disclosures with inconsistencies is unclear, and current readability research
does not inform us on how enhanced readability influences investors’ judgments when
there are inconsistencies in the disclosure.2 Indeed, communication theory indicates that
coherence or consistency is a key message attribute that people attend to, and influences
how people process the message (Kintsch and Van Dijk 1978), suggesting that the effects
of readability on investors’ judgments could vary depending on whether the
communicated message contains inconsistencies.

In this study, we examine how readability and benchmark performance consistency

to multiple benchmarks enables us to investigate whether improved readability has a
different impact on investors’ performance judgments when benchmark performance is
inconsistent versus consistent.
Building on the comprehension and persuasion literature, we predict that in the
presence of conflicting benchmark performance, investors can better understand trend
performance when readability is higher than when it is lower. Since a firm’s trend
performance is a more diagnostic cue of future performance (Abarbanell and Bushee
1997) relative to guidance performance, we posit that investors will make higher (lower)
performance judgments if they can better understand the positive (negative) trend
performance. As a result, higher readability (compared to lower readability) leads to
higher performance judgment in the positive trend performance condition, but lower
performance judgment in the negative trend performance condition. However, this effect
of readability on performance judgment through understanding is smaller when
benchmark performance is consistent.

We also investigate the mechanisms underlying the effects of readability on
performance judgment. Specifically, we examine whether the underlying mechanism
varies depending on benchmark performance consistency. Insights on this issue are
currently limited. Some studies assume that low readability obfuscates investors because
it impedes investors’ understanding of the disclosed information (Bloomfield 2002).
However, a recent study by Rennekamp (2012) finds no support for this mechanism;
instead, she finds support for processing fluency—the subjective feeling of the ease in
information processing—as a mediator. Our study attempts to provide and test a
theoretical account to reconcile these two perspectives. We note that one feature of
Rennekamp (2012) is that she examines a setting where there are no inconsistencies
within an earnings release, which makes it easier for investors to understand the message
regardless of readability. In this setting, readability serves to improve processing fluency.
However, our theory posits that the presence of inconsistencies makes it more difficult
for investors to understand the overall message and form an integrated perspective, thus
increasing the importance of readability in improving understanding and limiting its
impact on processing fluency.

II. LITERATURE REVIEW AND HYPOTHESIS DEVELOPMENT
Benchmark Performance Consistency
While there are many forms of inconsistencies in management disclosures (e.g.,
news relating to both positive and negative developments in revenue growth), we focus
on potential conflicting messages in terms of a firm’s current period performance relative
to different benchmarks. We focus on this aspect because managers often compare the
firm’s current quarter performance against different benchmarks such as prior guidance,
year-ago quarter performance, analyst’s consensus forecast, or last quarter performance
(Brown and Claylor 2005; Koonce and Lipe 2010). Managers also strategically
emphasize performance improvements relative to the chosen benchmarks (Krische 2005;
Schrand and Walther 2000). For example, managers may emphasize non-GAAP earnings
performance to portray a more favorable firm performance when the GAAP earnings
performance is unsatisfactory (Brown et al. 2005). The reference to different benchmarks
can lead to different evaluative outcomes (e.g., meeting one benchmark but missing
another), and to an overall message that contains inconsistent performance assessments

relative to different benchmarks (e.g., both better and worse performance depending on
which benchmark is referred to).
In this study, we consider two primary benchmarks—previously issued management
guidance and year-ago quarter performance. These benchmarks are important because
managers invariably compare current quarter performance with that in the year-ago
quarter, as well as previously issued guidance, if they have issued one.3 Guidance
performance is generally not perceived as objective and reliable as trend performance, in
that it is relatively easy for managers to strategically re-adjust their previously issued
guidance to enable actual current period earnings to meet/beat their most-recently issued
guidance (Cotter et al. 2006). In contrast, management has less control over trend
performance as it is more difficult to manipulate previously reported earnings numbers to
enable current period earnings to meet/beat previously reported earnings numbers. Trend
performance is also a more diagnostic indicator of future earnings performance
(Abarbanell and Bushee 1997).

Readability is the ease with which textual content can be read and understood (Dale
and Chall 1949). Prior studies find that readability affects investors’ reactions. For
example, You and Zhang (2009) find that low readability leads to investors’ under-
reactions to 10-K filings, and Miller (2010) finds that low readability reduces small
investors’ trading activities around the 10-K filing date. The cognitive difficulty in
processing low readability information has been proposed as an explanation in these

When benchmark performance is inconsistent, it is difficult for investors to arrive at
an integrated evaluation, as the firm’s guidance performance and trend performance
provide conflicting performance implications. In such cases, high readability facilities
investors’ information processing (Burgoon 1975; Masson and Waldron 1994), and helps
investors understand/distinguish performance evaluations against different benchmarks.
Accordingly, we expect that the presence of inconsistencies in a message will magnify
the role of readability on investors’ understanding of the message’s implications. In other
words, when benchmark performance is inconsistent, investors are more likely to form a
better integrated understanding of the firm’s current quarter performance relative to both
previously issued guidance and performance in the year-ago quarter when readability is
higher than when it is lower.

indicator of current period performance (and of future performance), managers’
discussion of guidance performance, which has an opposite directional implication on the
firm’s performance, can serve to hinder investors’ understanding of trend performance
and reduce its influence. In particular, low readability can limit investors’ understanding

Understanding vs. Processing Fluency
Rennekamp (2012) shows in an experimental study that the magnified valuation
changes in the high readability condition relative to the low readability condition operate
through high processing fluency rather than through improved understanding. One
feature in Rennekamp (2012) is that all the performance indicators communicate a
consistent performance message based on various comparisons (i.e., either increased
revenue and net profit with favorable outlook, or decreased revenue and net profit with
unfavorable outlook).
When all performance indicators have a consistent sign measured against different
benchmarks, investors likely have little difficulty in understanding the implications of the
indicators regardless of readability. On the other hand, processing fluency likely plays a
more significant role in explaining the effect of readability on investor judgment in this
case. As pointed out by Winkielman et al. (2012), fluency effects are more likely when
consistency exists. Specifically, consistency is about elements being tied together in a
coherent relation. Thus, a consistent message is more likely to activate the priming
mechanism, in which presentation of one element primes a related element (Winkielman
et al. 2012). Priming has been documented as one factor responsible for fluency effects

However, when performance evaluations relative to different benchmarks are

significant role in explaining the effect of readability on performance judgment.

propose the following two hypotheses:
H2a: When benchmark performance is consistent, processing fluency plays a more
significant role than understanding in explaining the effect of readability on performance
judgment.
H2b: When benchmark performance is inconsistent, understanding plays a more
significant role than processing fluency in explaining the effect of readability on
performance judgment.

Procedure
We design our case based on an actual company listed on the New York Stock
Exchange. All participants are told to assume the role of a general investor. They first
read some background information and historical financial data of the company. Next,
they read the earnings release, which has eight manipulated versions. Participants in each
condition receive one version of the earnings release. After reading the earnings release,
participants are asked to assess the firm’s future earnings potential, the stock price
appreciation potential, their likelihood to purchase the stock, and disclosure credibility.
Participants are then instructed that they cannot go back to previous pages once they
proceed to the next section. In the second section, participants recall and evaluate the
firm’s guidance performance and trend performance disclosed in the earnings release.
Finally, participants answer debriefing questions, manipulation check questions, and
provide demographic information in the third section.

VI. Experiment Two – Order Effect
A feature of management’s discussion of earnings is that earnings performance
relative to guidance is usually discussed first if the firm does issue guidance (Graham et
al. 2005). Hence, in our experiment, following actual practice, guidance performance is
discussed first followed by trend performance. However, it is unknown whether the
effects of readability on investors’ performance judgments and investment decisions
documented above still hold when trend performance is presented first followed by
guidance performance. Specifically, an alternative argument for our result is that
investors’ judgments are influenced by their understanding of trend performance not
because trend performance is more diagnostic, but because trend performance is
presented after guidance performance (i.e., a recency effect).
To address this question, we conduct a second experiment in which we manipulate
both presentation order (guidance performance first vs. trend performance first) and
readability (high vs. low). The experimental design is similar to that used in our main
experiment, except that we now focus on one specific inconsistent benchmark
performance situation—where current quarter performance is higher than year-ago
quarter performance but misses previously issued management guidance. We focus on

VII. CONCLUSION
In this study, we investigate how readability influences investor judgment and
whether this effect is contingent on benchmark performance consistency. Overall, we
find that readability influences investor judgment to a greater extent when benchmark
performance is inconsistent than when it is consistent. In the presence of conflicting
benchmark performance, compared to low readability, high readability helps investors
better understand trend performance, which in turn leads to higher performance judgment
when trend performance is positive, but lower performance judgment when trend
performance is negative. However, this effect of readability on performance judgment
through understanding is limited in the absence of conflicting benchmark performance. In
addition, we document that high readability enhances investors’ perceptions of disclosure
credibility and performance judgment through processing fluency, only when benchmark
performance is consistent but not when it is inconsistent.
Our study contributes to the literature on readability by identifying message
coherence (i.e., benchmark performance consistency) as a factor that moderates the
effects of readability on investor judgment. Our findings suggest that in assessing the
impact of readability, researchers and regulators should not only consider the surface
structure of a message (i.e., readability) but also the coherence of the message in that
these two attributes jointly affect investor judgment. Similarly, managers should also be
cautious about the use of a low readability strategy, since its impact is contingent on other
factors such as benchmark performance consistency.

Extant literature suggests that readability operates through a psychological
mechanism of processing fluency (Rennekamp 2012). Our study contributes to the
literature by identifying circumstances under which fluency as opposed to understanding
operates as the underlying mechanism of readability effects. We show that understanding
serves as a mediating mechanism through which readability affects investor judgment
when benchmark performance is inconsistent. In contrast, when benchmark performance
is consistent, processing fluency is the mechanism through which readability affects
investor judgment. These findings enrich our understanding on whether and how
readability helps investors make more informative judgments.
Further, we provide evidence on the benefits of improved readability, namely better
investor comprehension of inconsistencies within a disclosure and enhanced investors’
perceptions of disclosure credibility that can positively influence their performance
judgments. Both benefits are of interest to regulators as these have been alleged but
hitherto unverified assumptions made by the regulators (SEC 1998). The second benefit
is of interest to managers as well, since it suggests how they can influence investors’
performance judgments by improving disclosure credibility with high readability.
Our study has several limitations. First, we provide participants with short
management disclosures, when the volume of disclosures that investors encounter in
practice is significantly larger. While we believe that the effect of readability is magnified
when the disclosure becomes more voluminous, it is possible that investors lose focus
and become indifferent to readability in such a situation. Future research can investigate
this issue. Second, we examine only one particular context where there is benchmark
performance inconsistency (i.e., when there is contradictions in performance based on
