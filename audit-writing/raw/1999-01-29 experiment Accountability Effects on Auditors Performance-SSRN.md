Accountability effects on auditorsÕ performance:
influence of knowledge, problem-solving ability, and task complexity
1. Introduction
In this study, we examine three factors that may moderate the relation
between accountability (to a superior) and auditor performance: knowledge,
problem-solving ability, and task complexity. These factors correspond to
previously identified determinants of performance (e.g., Einhorn and Hogarth
[1980]); Libby and Luft [1993]). Specifically, we predict that accountability
works best when the requisite knowledge and abilities are matched with the
characteristics of the task.
Understanding the determinants of the accountability-performance relation
is important because accountability can be used by CPA firms to influence
auditorsÕ performance (through the review and performance evaluation processes).
Because a particular auditorÕs knowledge and problem-solving ability are
considered fixed at a given point in time, one of the few remaining ways a CPA
firm can influence auditor performance is to vary accountability demands.
Whether and how accountability may influence performance is currently
not very clear, and findings in the literature may sometimes appear contradictory.
For example, knowledge or experience differences among subject groups
sometimes moderated the effects of accountability (e.g., Kennedy [1993]; Cloyd
3

[1997]), but sometimes not (e.g., Chang et al. [1997]). Even when accountability
effects have been found to vary across subject groups, the nature of the interaction
is not clear. Kennedy [1993] found that accountability reduced the recency bias
for MBA students, but not for audit managers. On the other hand, Cloyd [1997]
found that accountability did not influence the search strategies of low knowledge
tax seniors but improved the strategies of high knowledge tax seniors. The studies
by Kennedy [1993] and Cloyd [1997] differ in several aspects such as task
complexity, subjects, and the knowledge required to perform the tasks, and it is
possible that these aspects, either alone or in combination, may account for the
different results.
Our study suggests that task complexity affects the way knowledge and
problem-solving ability interact with accountability. In our study, auditors
performed three tasks of varying complexity. In the low complexity task (listing
compliance and substantive tests), accountability did not influence performance.
As the task became more complex (listing financial statement errors associated
with an internal control deviation), accountability improved performance only
when knowledge was high. For the most complex task (listing causes associated
with a change in ratios), accountability improved performance only when both
knowledge and problem-solving ability were high. We interpret our results as
4

showing that accountability works best in particular combinations of knowledge,
problem-solving ability, and task complexity.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 contains the
background and hypothesis development. The experiment is described in Section
3, and the results are presented in Section 4. Section 5 concludes with a
discussion of the implications of the results and directions for future research.

We discuss three tasks of varying complexity to develop our framework
and hypotheses. Our view of task complexity is defined in terms of objective task
characteristics determined independently of the particular task-performer (Wood
[1986]; Campbell [1988]), so we avoid construct validity problems that arise from
defining task complexity as a function of person characteristics (see Wood [1986,
p. 62]; Campbell [1988, p. 50] for a discussion). We adopt WoodÕs [1986]
description of task complexity in terms of two aspects: (a) component
complexity, relating to the number of information cues to be processed and steps
7

to be executed in performing a task, and increasing with the number of cues and
steps; and (b) coordinative complexity, referring to the amount of coordination
required to perform the task, and increasing when steps in one part of the task
depend on steps taken earlier, when several related steps must be performed all at
once, and when backward-forward reasoning is required.2
Our low complexity task establishes a baseline condition where
accountability does not improve performance. Baseline identification has
implications for personnel assignment and for identifying conditions where CPA
firms may (or may not) want to increase accountability. The low complexity task
involves listing compliance and substantive tests in an internal control evaluation.
There is only one relevant information cue to be processed (control deviation for
liabilities) and there is no need to integrate multiple cues. The number of required
information processing acts (retrieval of the relevant tests from memory) is limited
because only a small and finite set of alternatives (compliance/substantive
procedures) needs to be considered. Coordinative complexity is also low because
there is little need to coordinate the sequence or timing of acts or to perform

backward/forward reasoning to retrieve the required output (unlike the ratio
analysis task, described later).
For this task, we expect the association between the required output
(compliance and substantive procedures) and the input (test for liabilities) to be
fairly strong in the auditorsÕ memories, given that they have extended exposure to
compliance/ substantive procedures (Abdolmohammadi [1991]; Bonner and
Pennington [1991]). Generation of the required output should therefore not
require significant effort or capacity usage in memory. Furthermore, the number
of cognitive acts required to perform the task is low. As a result, we anticipate
that accountability is unlikely to affect the performance of this task; our
prediction is stated formally in H1:
H1: For the low complexity task, accountability will not improve performance.
Our second task, of medium complexity, embodies a knowledge-
accountability interaction where accountability will improve performance for
auditors with high knowledge but not those with low knowledge. The task is part
of an internal control evaluation which requires the listing of financial statement
errors that can occur because of an internal control deviation. In terms of
component complexity, the number of separate acts required by this task
(retrieval of both the related financial statement errors and the double entries from
memory, plus the determination of whether the accounts are overstated or
9

understated) is higher than that required for the low complexity task (retrieval
from memory of compliance and substantive procedures). Component
complexity is also higher because the number of alternatives to be considered
(potential errors in a wide variety of accounts) is higher than the number for the
low complexity task (a more restricted set of compliance/substantive tests).
Information processing for this task is therefore likely to be more detailed and
complex than for the low complexity task. Like the low complexity task but
unlike the high complexity task (discussed later), coordinative complexity is low
and the task does not require much forward/backward reasoning; hence problem-
solving ability is not expected to influence performance (Bonner and Lewis
[1990]; Libby and Tan [1994]).3
Because we anticipate variation among auditors in the task-specific
knowledge they possess and bring to bear on this task, we expect knowledge to
moderate the accountability-performance relationship. We predict that
accountability will improve performance only for high knowledge auditors, i.e.,
those who have the requisite knowledge and are able to retrieve and apply it to the
task. Although both high and low knowledge auditors may be motivated to exert
greater effort as accountability increases, the performance of the low knowledge

Our high complexity task is a ratio analysis task. In terms of component
complexity, the number of input cues to be processed (three financial ratios) is
higher than required for the other two tasks (a control deviation), and in terms of
coordinative complexity, performance at one stage (listing of financial statement
errors) is contingent on prior performance (e.g., establishing the relationship
among the three ratios). Also, backward/forward reasoning is required because
there are interdependencies among the three ratios, and between each ratio and the
financial statement errors (i.e., a financial statement error must be consistent with
all three cues).

As in the medium complexity task, we expect knowledge to influence
performance. In addition, since the ratio analysis task has considerable
coordinative complexity and requires backward and forward reasoning, problem-
solving ability should influence performance (Bonner and Lewis [1990]; Libby and
Tan [1994]). However, we do not expect an interaction between knowledge and
accountability, because for this task, knowledge alone (even in the presence of
accountability) is insufficient. Instead, we expect a more complex interaction in
which accountability will lead to improved performance only when auditors have
the requisite attributes to perform the task. For auditors with low knowledge
and/or low problem-solving ability, accountability may make them work harder
but this additional effort will not translate to better performance. On the other
hand, accountability will make auditors with high knowledge and high problem-
solving ability exert the additional effort to retrieve and apply the requisite
knowledge and problem-solving skills to the task. In terms of the conceptual
model in Figure 1, panel C, we expect accountability (Link 1), knowledge (Link 2),
and problem-solving ability (Link 3) to influence performance, stated formally as
H3:

better performance when auditors have both high knowledge and

3. Method
3.1 OVERVIEW, SUBJECTS AND TASKS
Four independent variables were examined: task complexity,
accountability, knowledge and problem-solving ability. Task complexity was a
within-subject variable, manipulated by having subjects perform three tasks of
varying complexity. Accountability was manipulated between-subjects, with
subjects randomly assigned to either high or low accountability conditions.
SubjectsÕ knowledge and problem-solving ability were measured by knowledge
and ability tests. All subjects received the same materials, except for the
accountability manipulation, which was introduced just before they started the
experimental audit tasks. Subjects also completed a debriefing questionnaire
containing background information and manipulation check questions.
One hundred and five auditors from two Big Six firms in Singapore,
including 25 managers (mean experience = 73 months, standard deviation = 26
months), 26 seniors (mean experience = 41 months, standard deviation = 10
months), and 54 staff auditors (mean experience = 9 months, standard deviation =
7 months), participated in the study.
13

Subjects performed three tasks. The two internal control evaluation tasks
were adapted from the materials used in Bonner and Lewis [1990]. For the low
complexity task, subjects were required to list compliance tests to ascertain
whether the clientsÕ controls on payables and liabilities were effective, and to list
substantive tests to search for unrecorded liabilities. For the medium complexity
task, subjects were required to list financial statement errors that arise from
weaknesses in the clientÕs controls over the accounts payable system. Subjects
were required to determine the error, the accounts affected, and whether these
accounts would be overstated or understated. For the high complexity task,
subjects performed ratio analysis and were asked to list errors that could have
caused changes in some financial ratios. This task was modified from Libby
[1985] and Bonner and Lewis [1990]. For each task, subjects were instructed to
list as many solutions as possible.
Performance was measured by the number of correct responses. For the
task on compliance/substantive tests, we counted the number of correct
substantive and compliance tests listed. For the task on financial statement errors,
we counted the number of plausible errors listed. Plausible responses were those
that correctly stated how the error could occur, the accounts that would be
affected by such an error, and what the error would do to the magnitude of those
accounts. Implausible responses were those that did not fulfill all the criteria for
14

the plausible responses. For the ratio analysis task, we counted the number of
plausible errors listed. Plausible responses were those that explained all the ratio
changes in the right direction and those that were not specific but could be
possible errors. Implausible responses were those that explained the ratio changes
in the wrong direction, would not explain all the ratio changes, or were not
financial statement errors (e. g., business strategy changes).
One of the authors and an independent coder with five years of audit
experience coded the data. The independent coder was unaware of the
hypotheses. Across the three performance measures, the proportion of agreement
between the two judges was 95.1%, and the inter-rater reliability was 0.89 using
the Kappa coefficient (Cohen [1960]). Disagreements were resolved via
discussions between the two raters.
The knowledge test contained 16 multiple choice questions that tested
subjectsÕ knowledge of compliance tests (five questions), substantive tests (four
questions), financial statement errors (three questions), and ratio analysis (four
questions). Six questions were adapted from Bonner and Lewis [1990] and the
rest were from CPA examinations. SubjectsÕ problem-solving ability was
measured by questions from Thurstone [1957] and Sternberg [1988], eight of
which had been used previously in Tan and Libby [1997].
15

We measured the knowledge required for the low complexity task
(compliance/substantive tests) using the questions relating to compliance tests and
substantive tests. Since the medium complexity task (financial statement errors)
involved listing financial statement errors arising from a control deviation detected
during a test of controls, knowledge related to financial statement errors and
compliance tests would be most relevant. Knowledge required for this task was
measured using the financial statement error questions and the compliance test
questions. Finally, successful completion of the high complexity task should
require knowledge of financial statement errors and ratio analysis, which was
measured using the financial statement errors questions and the ratio analysis
questions.

3.3 PROCEDURE
All subjects received three sets of materials containing the knowledge and
problem-solving questions, the three audit tasks, and a debriefing questionnaire.
These materials were placed in separate sealed envelopes to prevent subjects from
reading the materials out of the intended sequence. All subjects received the same
materials, except for instructions relating to the accountability manipulation which
were attached to the front page of the package containing the performance tasks.
The high and low accountability conditions were administered in separate rooms.
Subjects completed the knowledge tests before they started on the audit
tasks, so that the accountability manipulation (introduced just before the start of
the three audit tasks) would not affect subjectsÕ effort level in completing the
knowledge and ability tests.
We also counterbalanced the order in which subjects performed the three
audit tasks. Subjects performed the ratio analysis task either before or after the
compliance/substantive task and the error-listing task. Subjects always performed
17

the compliance/substantive task before the error-listing task because these tasks
were related.5

5. DISCUSSION
In this study, we examined how the relation between accountability and
performance can be moderated by task complexity, knowledge, and problem-
solving ability, and provided evidence that accountability may not improve
performance for a low complexity task, for a medium complexity task where the
individual lacks the requisite knowledge, and for a high complexity task where the

23

individual lacks either the requisite knowledge or problem-solving ability. The
implication of these findings is that accountability may induce increased effort but
not improved performance, if the auditor lacks aptitude for a task and has reached
his/her maximum level of competence. In such an instance, it may be more helpful
to introduce decision aids, to replace the auditor with someone with the requisite
knowledge/ability, and/or to re-assign the auditor to a task which matches his/her
knowledge/ability. Regardless of whether the auditor lacks knowledge required for
a task, accountability may still be useful if the objective is to encourage learning,
provided appropriate learning opportunities are available; however, learning
opportunities for some tasks are sometimes less than might be desired for efficient
learning (see Bonner and Pennington [1990]).
Results from related studies can also be integrated using the framework
described in this paper. For example, in KennedyÕs [1993] study, the audit
managers probably knew how to avoid recency effects, and applying their
knowledge to the task was not particularly effortful for them; hence,
accountability did not matter, as in the case of the low complexity task in our
study. Ashton [1990] had auditors perform a bond rating task, and found that
with increased task complexity (operationalized as the presence of a decision aid),
the need to justify decisions had a detrimental effect on performance. Note that
the auditors in AshtonÕs [1990] study did not have experience in bond rating (and
24

may not have the requisite task-specific knowledge). It is therefore possible that
the justification pressure encouraged subjects to reduce reliance on the decision aid
and increase reliance on their own judgment; however, because they did not have
the relevant knowledge for the task, performance suffered. This context is similar
to the low knowledge subjects in our study performing the medium complexity
task, where we found that high accountability led to poorer performance. In a
recent study, Chang et al. [1997] had undergraduate and MBA students, who
presumably differed in statistical knowledge, perform an ability-sensitive task.
They did not include ability as an independent variable, and found no
accountability-knowledge interaction. This context is similar to our high
complexity task (that was sensitive to variations in both knowledge and problem-
solving ability), where our results indicated no interaction between accountability
and knowledge unless we controlled for problem-solving ability. Since ability was
an omitted variable in Chang et al.Õs [1997] study, it is possible that the
moderating role of knowledge had been suppressed in that study.
Finally, on a methodological note, our findings suggest that researchers
using accountability to influence task performance should consider whether there
is a match between the task complexity and the knowledge and ability of the
subjects. Accountability may not be effective if task characteristics do not match
25

subject attributes. In addition, even if there are no such mismatches, researchers
may be unable to detect accountability effects if there are omitted variables.
