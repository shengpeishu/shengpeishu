Who Likes Jargon? The Joint Effect of Jargon Type and Industry Knowledge on
Investors’ Judgments
ABSTRACT: We conduct an experiment to examine how jargon affects investment willingness
for investors with different levels of industry knowledge and whether such effects vary with
good or bad jargon. We find that with respect to investors without industry knowledge, jargon
decreases investment willingness because it decreases understanding. However, with respect to
investors with some-but-low industry knowledge, jargon increases investment willingness
because it increases perceived product premium. Such effects exist regardless of whether good or
bad jargon is used. In contrast, investors with high industry knowledge differentiate between
good and bad jargon and reduce investment willingness only when bad jargon is used. These
findings have implications for regulators, managers, and investors.

1. Introduction
Jargon, which refers to the use of technical or obscure language (Merriam-Webster, 2003),
is an important feature that affects the readability of management disclosures (Bonsall et al.,
2017). The amount of jargon used in management disclosures varies among companies, and such
variations exist even among companies in the same industry with similar business models and
product lines.1 In this study, we investigate how the use of jargon in management disclosures
alters investors’ willingness to invest in a stock. Specifically, we investigate whether the effect of
jargon on investment judgments varies with investors’ industry knowledge, and whether such
effects are contingent on the type of jargon (good jargon versus bad jargon).
The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)’s Plain English Handbook (SEC,
1998) states that jargon makes it difficult for investors to understand management disclosures.
This is consistent with survey findings that too much jargon gets in the way of clarity and makes
information hard to understand for non-professional investors (SEC, 2009), a sentiment also
shared by professional investors (FRC, 2009; KPMG, 2013). While the SEC’s Plain English
Handbook (SEC, 1998) suggests that managers avoid business and technical jargon in their
disclosures, managers have not limited their use of jargon (Accounting WEB, 2011). One
possibility for managers’ use of jargon is that they intend to signal their competence and
expertise to attract more investment. This is possible as psychological research shows that people
consider jargon a signal of an author’s expertise (Sawyer et al., 2008) and believe that complex
words are indicative of a more intelligent author (Pennebaker and King, 1999). Given these
competing views, it is important to examine whether and how management’s use of jargon
affects investor judgment.
One factor that could potentially moderate the effect of jargon is investors’ industry
knowledge, as previous research suggests that knowledge is an important factor that affects
judgment and decision making (Ericsson and Lehman, 1996; Ericsson and Smith, 1991;
Frederick and Libby, 1986; Libby and Luft, 1993). Communication research suggests that jargon
is perceived differently by different readers. Specifically, readers who have the knowledge to

Another factor that could influence the effect of jargon is jargon type. According to the
definitions of jargon in Merriam-Webster (2003), there are two types of jargon: (1) the technical
terminology or characteristic idiom of a special activity or group; and (2) obscure and often
pretentious language marked by circumlocutions and long words. Nash (1993) distinguishes the
“uses” and “abuses” of jargon, whereby the term “uses” means the legitimate use of jargon (also
known as shop talk or good jargon), and the term “abuses” means the pretentious use of jargon
(also known as show talk or bad jargon). Despite the fact that jargon can be good or bad (Hirst,
2003), capital market regulators seem to have concerns over jargon at a generic level, without
differentiating between the uses and abuses of jargon. However, some investors (especially those
with high industry knowledge) may well appreciate good jargon, which is, on occasions, “a
necessary evil” to facilitate efficient and precise communication of technical knowledge
(Rickard, 1908). Thus, it is important for managers to understand the different effects of good
and bad jargon on investors’ judgments to facilitate information dissemination and attract more
capital market investment.

manipulation and further validate the jargon manipulation with a panel of experts. We measure
industry knowledge based on the number of biology, biomedicine, and other related courses that
participants have taken. Finally, we ask participants to indicate their willingness to invest in the
stock and the attractiveness of the stock, and we use the average of these two measures as our
main dependent variable—investment willingness.
We conduct an analysis of variance (ANOVA) test with jargon type and industry
knowledge as the independent variables and investment willingness as the dependent variable.
The interaction effect of jargon type and industry knowledge is significant, suggesting that the
impact of jargon on investment willingness is contingent on industry knowledge. We then
conduct separate contrast tests for investors with various levels of industry knowledge. In terms
of investors without industry knowledge, we find that investment willingness is the highest in the
no jargon condition, followed by the good or bad jargon alone condition, and the lowest in the
both-good-and-bad jargon condition, consistent with our prediction that jargon (regardless of
whether it is good or bad) reduces investment willingness. To understand the underlying causal
mechanism, we conduct a mediation analysis and find that jargon reduces these investors’
investment willingness because it decreases their understanding. In contrast, with respect to
investors with some-but-low industry knowledge, investment willingness is the lowest in the no
jargon condition, followed by the good or bad jargon alone condition, and the highest in the
both-good-and-bad jargon condition. This result is consistent with our prediction that jargon
(regardless of whether it is good or bad) increases investment willingness. Furthermore, the
mediation analysis indicates that jargon makes these investors accord more premium toward the
company’s products, which in turn increases their investment willingness. Last, in terms of

managers to be more credible when more jargon is used, investors with high industry knowledge

low industry knowledge is dampened when syntactic complexity is higher than when it is lower.
However, the effect of jargon on investors with high industry knowledge does not vary with
levels of syntactic complexity.
Our study contributes to the recent accounting literature that identifies jargon as an
important linguistic attribute but has not examined its impact on investors’ judgments (Bonsall et
al., 2017). We propose and demonstrate that the use of jargon in management disclosures can
lead to either positive or negative investor reaction, which is contingent on jargon type and
investors’ industry knowledge. Regarding investors with little industry knowledge or those
without industry knowledge, jargon type does not matter, but the presence of jargon affects these
investors in opposite directions. Specifically, we find that while the presence of jargon decreases
investment willingness for investors without industry knowledge, it increases investment
willingness for investors with some-but-low industry knowledge. These findings counter the
SEC’s assumption that investors with different levels of knowledge behave similarly to jargon
(SEC, 1998), and suggest that it is important to consider investors’ industry knowledge when
determining the use of jargon in management disclosures. In contrast, jargon type matters for

investors with high industry knowledge; they reduce their investment willingness only when bad

In addition, our paper demonstrates that jargon could affect investors’ judgments through
either understanding or processing disfluency, which is determined by investors’ industry
knowledge. Investors with high industry knowledge can understand jargon and assess whether
jargon is good or bad, and thus form their investment judgments based on their assessments of
the appropriate use of jargon. Investors without industry knowledge make investment judgment
based on the extent to which they can understand management disclosures containing jargon. In
contrast, investors with some-but-low industry knowledge have difficulty telling the source of
disfluency they experience, causing them to believe disfluency is diagnostic about the message
conveyed in management disclosures and thus rely more on processing disfluency to make their
judgments. This could reflect a heuristic judgment strategy that investors with some-but-low
industry knowledge adopt, resulting in potentially irrational behavior such as overly optimistic
reactions to management disclosures containing jargon (Ljungqvist, 2007).
Last, our study provides some evidence consistent with the information asymmetry
explanation for IPO underpricing. As a result of the strict disclosure rules in the U.S., IPO
underwriters and issuers face considerable risk of litigation if material facts in IPO prospectuses
are misstated or omitted (Ljungqvist, 2007; Lowry and Shu, 2002). In order to minimize
litigation risk and communicate industry-specialized information more precisely and accurately,
underwriters and issuers may intentionally choose to use jargon in IPO prospectuses, which
could exaggerate the information asymmetry problem. According to the information asymmetry
explanation for IPO underpricing (Ritter 1984; Beatty and Ritter 1986), if underwriters and
issuers anticipate that uninformed investors will avoid investing in the stock when information
asymmetry is high, they have to underprice IPO stocks to encourage more investment from such
investors (Rock, 1986). Consistent with the anticipations of underwriters and issuers, we find
that the presence of jargon (which increases information asymmetry) does reduce the investment
willingness for investors without industry knowledge. In addition, when bad jargon makes
information vague, complicated, and pretentious (Hirst, 2003), resulting in larger information
asymmetry, we find that investors with high industry knowledge will also reduce investment
willingness. Overall, our results suggest that investment willingness is lower for both uninformed

2. Theory and Hypotheses
Communication research classifies jargon into two categories: well-used jargon and badly-
used jargon (Hirst, 2003).3 Jargon is well used when it is well adapted to its audience and
necessary to achieve the goal of efficient and precise communication. In contrast, jargon is badly
used when it is ill-adapted to its audience or whenever jargon is excessive or unnecessary (Hirst,
2003). Following this argument, we identify two factors that could affect investors’ perceptions
of jargon and their subsequent investment decisions: (1) jargon type, which relates to whether the
jargon used in management disclosures is necessary (hereafter, good jargon) or unnecessary
(hereafter, bad jargon); and (2) investors’ industry knowledge, which determines whether the use
of jargon in management disclosures is well- or ill-adapted to its audience. We classify investors’
industry knowledge into three levels: no industry knowledge, some-but-low industry knowledge,
and high industry knowledge, and consider investors to be those with higher industry knowledge
if they have accumulated knowledge in an industry via more education experience or more
related working experience.
While communication research suggests that jargon can affect people’s understanding of
information (Nash, 1993), psychological research indicates that jargon can also affect an
individual’s subjective feeling about how difficult it is to process information, i.e., processing
disfluency (Oppenheimer, 2006). Recent disfluency research shows that individuals with
different personal backgrounds or attributes can have different perceptions when they experience
disfluency (Galak and Nelson, 2011; Hélène et al., 2013; Labroo et al., 2009; Miele and Molden,
2010). Oppenheimer (2008) suggests that how processing disfluency is interpreted depends on

show that people do not use processing disfluency as a cue for their judgment when its
informational value is called into question; for example, when processing disfluency is easily
traced back to one’s own state of knowledge (Schwarz, 2004). Specifically, Tybout et al. (2005)

individuals’ judgments are based on information content. However, when task-relevant
knowledge is between these two extremes of accessibility, judgments are based on processing
disfluency. Below, we discuss how different types of jargon affect investors with no industry
knowledge, some-but-low industry knowledge, and high industry knowledge, respectively.
2.1.Investors without Industry Knowledge
According to Tybout et al. (2005), when task-relevant knowledge is not at all accessible,
individuals tend to attribute the subjective feeling of difficulty in information processing (i.e.,
processing disfluency) to their lack of knowledge. In our context, we expect that investors
without industry knowledge will experience difficulty in understanding the content when they
are exposed to jargon in management disclosures. Exposure to jargon and the associated
difficulty remind them that they do not have the required knowledge related to the industry. This
reduces their understanding of the content they read, and decreases their investment willingness.
Moreover, due to their lack of industry knowledge, we do not expect these investors to be able to
assess whether the use of jargon is good or bad in management disclosures; hence, holding
constant the amount of jargon usage, we predict similar effects when either bad jargon or good
jargon is present. In other words, we expect these investors to react merely to the presence of
jargon rather than the type of jargon. Accordingly, we predict that investment willingness is
higher when either good or bad jargon is absent, but lower when either good or bad jargon is
present.
Our predictions above do not distinguish between whether the judgment effects of bad
jargon occur in isolation (i.e., there is only bad jargon but no good jargon present) or in the
concurrent presence of good jargon (i.e., there is both good and bad jargon), and vice versa in the
case of the judgment effects of good jargon. In the case of investors without industry knowledge,
our premise is that these investors cannot differentiate between good and bad jargon. However, a
practical reality when both good and bad jargon is used is that the amount of jargon usage is also

likely to increase relative to the use of either jargon alone. The reason for this is that bad jargon
often involves the inclusion of highly-technical firm-specific terms (e.g., “our basal medium
optimization technology”) with no corresponding good-jargon equivalent (i.e., the alternative is
to simply refer to “our technology”). As a result, including both good and bad jargon in
management disclosures can increase the amount of jargon used. In this case, compared to a
disclosure containing either bad jargon or good jargon alone, a disclosure that has both good and
bad jargon will be construed by investors without industry knowledge to be one that has more
jargon and that is less understandable. Accordingly, we predict that the effects of either bad or
good jargon in isolation will be magnified in the presence of both good and bad jargon, which
essentially involves more jargon.4 Combined, our first hypothesis is formally stated as follows:
H1: Investment willingness for investors without industry knowledge is highest in the
absence of jargon, followed by the presence of either bad or good jargon alone, and lowest in the
presence of both bad and good jargon.
2.2.Investors with Some-but-Low Industry Knowledge
Based on Tybout et al. (2005), when task-relevant knowledge is between the extreme of
not at all accessible and the extreme of highly accessible, the cause of processing disfluency
cannot be easily attributed to the state of knowledge. As a result, individuals tend to believe
processing disfluency to be informative about the target object, and they rely on it to make their
judgments. In our study, investors with some-but-low industry knowledge likely feel difficulty in
processing management disclosures, but they are reluctant to attribute such difficulty to their
insufficient levels of knowledge. Rather, these investors tend to believe their feelings of
difficulty has information value for their investment judgments.

read descriptions of special or unique products (Pocheptsova et al., 2010). Specifically,
Pocheptsova et al. (2010) find that while people prefer an easy-to-process description to a
difficult-to-process description for an everyday cheese product, their preferences reverse for a
special occasion cheese product as the difficulty of processing matches the feelings of the

2.3.Investors with High Industry Knowledge
Following Tybout et al. (2005), who suggest that individuals’ judgments are based on
information content when task-relevant knowledge is highly accessible, we expect that investors
with high industry knowledge will make their judgments based on their assessments of
management’s use of jargon in their disclosures. Since investors with high industry knowledge
can understand jargon contained in management disclosures, they are able to assess whether the
jargon used is good or bad. While the use of jargon is well-adapted for investors with high
industry knowledge, the excessive or unnecessary use of jargon could still be considered as “bad
jargon” (Hirst, 2003). In psychological research, Oppenheimer (2006) also finds that using
needlessly long words has a negative impact on evaluations of the authors’ competence. He also
provides evidence that individuals give lower evaluations for essays with needlessly long words
because these essays are unnecessarily difficult to read. Hence, we predict that investors with
high industry knowledge will consider bad jargon to be unnecessary and that their investment
willingness will decrease when bad jargon is present.
Because investors with high knowledge disdain management’s use of bad jargon, investors
likely react negative in the bad jargon condition compared to the no jargon condition. In fact, the
mere presence of bad jargon, regardless of whether good jargon is present or not, is likely
sufficient to induce negative judgments among these investors. Note that this prediction holds
even when the total amount of jargon usage increases in the presence of both good and bad
jargon, since investors with high industry knowledge are expected to react to the mere presence
of bad jargon. Hence, we expect that the presence of both bad and good jargon will not magnify
the impact of bad jargon alone for investors with high industry knowledge. We formally state our
H3a as follows:
H3a: Investment willingness for investors with high industry knowledge is higher in the
absence of bad jargon than in its presence, regardless of the presence or absence of good
jargon.

There is ambiguity in terms of how good jargon affects investment willingness for investors
with high industry knowledge. On one hand, these investors may recognize the communication
efficiency associated with the use of good jargon and place a premium on firms that are able to
judiciously utilize such good jargon in their disclosures. On the other hand, it may also be that
these investors expect such good jargon to be used, and therefore no premium is placed when
managers use such good jargon. As a result, we do not make a directional prediction on the effect
of good jargon on the investment willingness for investors with high industry knowledge.
Recall that in H3a, we predict that investors with high industry knowledge will have lower
investment willingness in the presence of both bad and good jargon compared to the case when
no jargon is present. Given that we do not expect a difference between no jargon and the
presence of good jargon alone, we expect the presence of both bad and good jargon (i.e., a
situation where bad jargon is present) will result in lower investment willingness compared to the
good jargon alone condition as well. We formally state H3b as follows:
H3b: Investment willingness for investors with high industry knowledge is higher in the
presence of good jargon alone compared to the presence of both bad and good jargon.
3. Experiment
3.1.Participants and Case Material
We recruit 284 PMBA students from a major U.S. university to participate in our
experiment. Each participant is compensated with a $20 Amazon Gift Card. On average, our
participants have taken 2.47 accounting courses and 2.26 finance courses, and they have 7.25
years of work experience. About 88 percent of them have previously read annual reports and/or
earnings releases. These attributes are comparable to the retail investors recruited in Elliott et al.
(2007), who have taken an average of 1.9 accounting courses and 1.6 finance courses, and 97
percent of them have previously evaluated financial statements. Hence, our PMBA participants
are appropriate proxies for investors.
One feature of the PMBA program from which we recruit participants is that a large
proportion of its students are doctors, physicians, or people who hold Doctor of Medicine (MD),
biology, or chemistry degrees. Thus, we capitalize on this unique feature of the program, and
design our case based on a real biotech company, given the increasing importance of healthcare

4.2.2. How does Jargon Affect Investors’ Credibility Judgment?
Psychological studies suggest that jargon could increase credibility judgment as it suggests a
higher level of management competence and a higher level of exerted effort (Thompson and
Ince, 2013). With respect to investors with no industry knowledge, it is possible that they believe
management is the expert and thus rely on the use of jargon to infer management credibility. It is
also possible that these investors do not make different credibility assessments based on jargon
because they do not perceive that they have the knowledge to assess credibility. With respect to
investors with some-but-low industry knowledge, as discussed in our theory section, they are
more likely to rely on heuristic cues to make judgments. More particularly to an IPO setting,
since management is motivated to reduce information asymmetry in order to reduce IPO

incentives at IPO and conclude that management is correspondingly more credible (Koonce et
al., 2016). Thus, investors with some-but-low knowledge will perceive managers to be more
credible with more jargon used in the IPO prospectus. However, such an effect of jargon on
credibility is less likely for investors with high industry knowledge, given that they have the
requisite knowledge to complete a task and are less likely to rely on simple cues to infer
credibility (Ericsson and Lehmann, 1996; Ericsson and Smith, 1991; Frederick and Libby, 1986;
Libby and Luft, 1993).
In order to measure investors’ credibility judgments, we ask participants to assess
management competence and trustworthiness (Mercer, 2005). Both questions are measured on
11-point scales with “0” labeled “not at all competent/trustworthy” and “10” labeled “absolutely

5. Discussion and Conclusion
We investigate how the effects of jargon on investors’ investment willingness are
moderated by industry knowledge and jargon type. In an experiment with PMBA students, we
find that including more jargon in management disclosures reduces investment willingness for
investors without industry knowledge, but increases investment willingness for investors with
low industry knowledge; these effects exist regardless of whether good or bad jargon is used.
However, with respect to investors with high industry knowledge, they do differentiate good
jargon from bad jargon, and their investment willingness is reduced only when bad jargon is
present but not when good jargon is present. We also conduct another experiment to investigate
the moderating role of syntactic complexity on jargon impact. Our results suggest that while the
effect of jargon on investors with low industry knowledge varies with syntactic complexity, its
impact on investors with high industry knowledge prevails irrespective of syntactic complexity.
Our study contributes to recent literature examining the effect of linguistic features on
investors’ judgments. Prior studies have examined the effects of readability, tone, and language
concreteness on investors’ judgments and decisions (Elliott et al., 2015; Hwang and Kim, 2017;
Rennekamp, 2012; Tan et al., 2014, 2015). Specifically, Hwang and Kim (2017) examine the
effect of readability on firm valuation and finds that difficult-to-read reports decrease firm value.
Our paper is different from prior readability studies in that we specifically identify and document
the effect of jargon (one particular element of readability) on investor judgment, while holding
other attributes of readability (e.g., passive verbs, hidden verbs, overwriting, and wordy phrases)
constant. More importantly, we identify three different groups of investors with various levels of
industry knowledge and show that jargon effect varies with industry knowledge. In addition, one

particular feature of the impact of jargon is that different investors could interpret management’s

also on the type of jargon and an overall contextual linguistic attribute (i.e., syntactic

jargon type, industry knowledge, and syntactic complexity on investors’ judgments. Our findings
help managers better understand the implications of including jargon (and different types of
jargon) in their disclosures, and they can also better understand the impact of their disclosure
choices on their primary investor base.
As discussed in our results, jargon will reduce investment willingness for investors
without industry knowledge due to their lower understanding of management disclosures, but
increase investment willingness for investors with low industry knowledge because it induces
feelings of product premium. Managers need to understand these contrasting effects. Compared
to managers who incorporate more jargon in their disclosures, well-meaning managers who
follow the prescription of the SEC in using less jargon in their public disclosures may attract
more investment from investors without industry knowledge but less investment from investors
with low industry knowledge. Correspondingly, investors, especially those with some but not
sufficient levels of industry knowledge, should be careful in terms of not getting unduly
influenced by managers’ use of jargon and technical terms. Our results also show that investors
with high industry knowledge can tell good jargon from bad jargon, and their investment
willingness is reduced with the presence of bad jargon, which is considered unnecessary in
management disclosures. This challenges the conventional wisdom that managers can use more
jargon in disclosures targeted at investors with more knowledge, as our results suggest that more
bad jargon may actually reduce investment willingness for such investors.
Our study examines investors’ investment willingness only in the case of an IPO. We use
an IPO prospectus summary as our experimental setting because managers often use more jargon
to describe their companies and businesses in such disclosures. While we expect similar results
for tasks that require knowledge in other fields, future research could look at other disclosure
settings such as conference calls where managers use jargon in discussing their firm
performance. Another feature of an IPO prospectus summary is that it usually does not contain
much financial performance information, which helps us to first establish the effect of jargon in a

clean setting without other confounding factors. Specifically, if our setting contains more
financial performance information, it is possible that investors will over-rely on financial
performance information and ignore the qualitative information, especially when they do not
understand jargon. Still, it is not clear whether jargon will have similar effects in the presence of
financial performance information, and this is an issue which can be examined in future studies.
In addition, it is also unknown whether the jargon effect documented in our paper will be
dampened in cases where management is able to do roadshows and live presentations. For
example, if investors expect that they can find out more about the company through roadshows,
those who cannot comprehend the jargon are more likely to rely on other information channels
when evaluating the firm, rather than responding to a jargon-ridden IPO prospectus. Future
research can examine these issues.
