 1. Introduction

 Both the popular press and research literature report increasing use of

 short-horizon earnings guidance (referred to hereafter as "warnings"),
 primarily before negative earnings surprises.1 While it is claimed that in-

 vestors and analysts have "little tolerance for disappointments," feel "mis-

 led" by the failure to warn, lose confidence in management, and "vote

 with their feet" by selling off their shares in companies which do not

 warn, Kasznik and Lev [1995] reported findings suggesting the opposite.

 Specifically, they found that, controlling for the magnitude of the nega-

 tive earnings surprise, firms that warn tend to experience more negative

 stock price reactions, and more negative analyst earnings forecast revi-

 sions, than those providing no warning.

 In this paper, we investigate analysts' reactions to qualitative warnings

 of adverse earnings and attempt to reconcile analysts' more negative fore-

 cast revisions, as documented by Kasznik and Lev [1995], and the appar-

 ently conflicting anecdotal evidence that suggests more positive responses

 to firms that warn. We conjecture that the inconsistency arises because

 warnings cause analysts to revise their earnings forecasts sequentially in

 response to two related signals (the warning and the earnings announce-

 ment). However, their responses to retrospective questions about the ef-

 fects of warnings are the result of a simultaneous response to both signals.

 Several findings in psychology suggest that the sequential response to in-

 formation may cause analysts' forecast revisions to be more negative than

 implied by their stated beliefs, resulting from the simultaneous response

 to the same information.

 As noted above, most warnings are issued before large negative earn-

 ings surprises and tend to be qualitative (Skinner [1994], Kasznik and

 Lev [1995], and Bamber and Cheon [1998]). Therefore, we focus on this

 particular type of warning. Our experiment was designed to test whether

 the act of sequentially processing a warning followed by an earnings an-

 nouncement is a potential explanation for the conflict between the neg-

 ative effect of warning on analysts' forecasts documented in Kasznik and

 Lev [1995] and the positive effect suggested by the anecdotal evidence.

 In our experiment, 28 financial analysts predicted future years' earnings

 in one of three conditions. The first two conditions simulated the actual

 corporate disclosure environment. In the No Warning condition, analysts

 received an earnings announcement, but no warning, after which they

 adjusted their estimates of future years' earnings. In the Sequential Warn-

 ing condition, the analysts received the warning first, adjusted their future

 earnings estimates conditional on the warning, and then received the

 earnings announcement, after which they made a second adjustment to

 their future years' estimates.9 The third condition, which could not exist

 in the naturally occurring environment, required the same single-step

 process as the No Warning condition but provided the same information

 about the firm as the Sequential Warning condition. In the Simultaneous

 Warning condition, analysts received both the warning and actual earn-

 ings announcement at the same time, and made a single adjustment in

 response to both signals.

 The comparison between the No Warning and Simultaneous Warning

 conditions allowed us to assess whether, without the act of sequential pro-

 cessing, the warnings were evaluated positively or negatively. The compar-

 ison between the Simultaneous Warning and Sequential Warning conditions

 allowed us to determine whether the act of sequential processing contrib-

 uted to a more negative effect for the warning. The comparison between

 the No Warning and Sequential Warning conditions indicated how the ef-

 fects of warning could combine with sequential processing to provide a

 potential explanation for the results in Kasznik and Lev [1995].

 Analysts in all three conditions predicted substantial declines in future

 earnings based on the information presented. Furthermore, the forecast

 revisions in both stages of the Sequential Warning condition were nega-

 tive, indicating that the actual earnings decline in our experiment was

 greater than the analysts expected given the qualitative warning alone.

 Consistent with the anecdotal evidence suggesting that analysts evaluate

 warnings positively, analysts in the Simultaneous Warning condition fore-

 casted higher future earnings than analysts in the No Warning condition.

 However, analysts in the Sequential Warning condition forecasted much

 lower future earnings than those in the Simultaneous Warning condition.

 This suggests that, when the actual earnings decline is greater than ex-

 pected given the qualitative warning alone, the act of sequential process-

 ing may contribute to the negative responses to warnings documented in

 Kasznik and Lev [1995]. The warning was clearly weighted differently in

 the Sequential and Simultaneous Warning conditions. Consistent with the

 empirical-archival results, analysts in the Sequential Warning condition

 forecasted slightly lower future earnings than in the No Warning condi-

 tion. Taken together, these results suggest that any potential positive im-

 pact of analysts' stated beliefs about the firms that warn (as reflected in

 their responses to retrospective questions) could be more than offset by

 the effects of sequentially processing a warning followed by an earnings

 announcement.

 Our debriefing was designed to provide a context similar to a reporter's

 retrospective request to compare firms that do and do not warn. The re-

 sults of our debriefing were consistent with the anecdotal evidence. Our

 participating analysts indicated that if a company warned of adverse earn-

 ings, management had more integrity and the earnings decline would be

 less persistent; the analysts were more likely to continue coverage and to

 hold the company's securities and less likely to lower their estimates of

 future earnings; and the analysts' own reputations would be damaged less

 than if the same company had failed to warn. The debriefing results were

 also consistent with the differences in forecasts provided in our No Warn-

 ing and Simultaneous Warning conditions, supporting our suggestion that

 the Simultaneous Warning condition captures the same behavior exhibited

 when analysts answer retrospective questions.

 2.1 WARNINGS OF ADVERSE EARNINGS SURPRISES AND THE

 MARKET RESPONSE

 Previous research on the market's reaction to warnings has found that

 warnings exacerbate the stock price effect of the earnings surprise, con-

 trolling for the magnitude of the surprise (Skinner [1994], Francis, Phil-

 brick, and Schipper [1994], and Kasznik and Lev [1995]). Most closely

 closures made by firms facing large earnings surprises between the 31st

 day after the third-quarter earnings announcement and the fourth-quar-

 ter earnings announcement. They examined 64 matched pairs of "bad

 news" firms with a negative fourth-quarter earnings surprise, relative to

 analysts' forecasts, that exceeded 1% of stock price during 1988, 1989, or

 nouncement. The combined return over the five days surrounding the

 warning and the five days surrounding the earnings announcement was

 significantly more negative for the warning firms, controlling for the mag-

 nitude of unexpected earnings and size. Most relevant to our experiment,

 the Value Line forecast revisions for year t + 1 were also more negative

 for the warning firms.3 The authors noted that these findings were

 "counterintuitive because a warning generally provides partial informa-

 tion about the subsequent earnings surprise, and is therefore expected

 to be rewarded by investors" (Kasznik and Lev [1995, p. 128]). Our study

 focuses on the modal form of warning in their sample, qualitative warn-

 ings of large negative surprises.

 Relatedly, Soffer, Thiagarajan, and Walther [forthcoming] investigated

 all quantitative earnings-related warnings issued during the time interval

 15 days before the end of a quarter and the day before the quarterly earn-

 ings announcement during the period September 1992 to December

 1995, regardless of the size of the earnings surprise. Although they ana-

 lyzed a different type of warning and did not examine analysts' forecasts,

 their analysis of the effect of the percentage of the total earnings news

 disclosed by the preannouncement is relevant to our study. They pre-

 dicted that firms disclosing less than 100% of the bad news in the prean-

 nouncement would experience significantly lower combined returns in

 response to the preannouncement and actual earnings announcement

 than those disclosing 100% or more, controlling for the magnitude of the

 total earnings surprise. In the former case, as in our study of qualitative

 warnings, both the preannouncement and the earnings announcements

 contained bad news. Their results concerning the effects of warnings of

 negative earnings surprises support our findings and our suggestion that

 it is the sequential processing of two pieces of bad news that causes the

 more negative average market response to firms that warn.

 evidence. Also, because they examined naturally occurring phenomena,

 neither study could effectively control for other differences between firms

 that do and do not warn. In our experiment, we were able to hold other

 2.2 ANALYSTS' PREFERENCES FOR WARNINGS

 A portion of the literature focuses on managers' possible motives for

 warning of bad news. Skinner [1994] suggested two possible motives: (1)

 to reduce the risk of shareholder litigation, and (2) to maintain a good

 reputation with analysts who will not follow or recommend firms which

 fail to warn. While Skinner [1994; 1997] and Francis, Philbrick, and

 Schipper [1994] focused on the litigation risk question, we are aware of

 no studies which examine the second explanation.

 Analysts' preference for warnings or "earnings guidance" is supported

 by Tasker's [1999] content analysis of conference calls. Our conjecture is

 that analysts believe their reputations will be damaged less if they adjust

 their future periods' earnings estimates based on an earlier warning as

 opposed to an earnings announcement. We suspect that these reputa-

 tional concerns, in turn, are reflected in their beliefs about the firm in-

 volved (including the integrity of company management), their stated

 willingness to continue to provide coverage, and their stated expecta-

 tions about the persistence of the earnings decline.

 However, the actual cost to firms of failing to warn is less than clear.

 Skinner [1994] suggests that failure to warn will lead to reduced analyst

 following and liquidity. The popular press suggests that this will lead to

 a sell-off of shares and a lower stock price. However, the empirical-archi-

 val studies cited above suggest a more negative stock price reaction to

 negative earnings surprises preceded by a warning, and Kasznik and Lev

 [ 1995] found more negative forecast revisions for warning firms. The fail-

 ure of prior research to document any advantage resulting from acceding

 to analysts' wishes suggests the possibility of a disconnect between the an-

 ecdotes describing analysts' stated beliefs and their actions reflected in

 their forecast revisions.

 2.3 COGNITIVE PROCESSING OF WARNINGS

 We suggest that warnings are processed in fundamentally different ways

 when analysts answer retrospective questions and when they revise earn-

 ings forecasts in reaction to warnings. There are many reasons that these

 different processes may lead to more negative reactions to warnings in the

 forecast revision setting.

 As noted earlier, warnings of negative earnings surprises include a neg-

 ative element (the underlying news about earnings) and a positive element

 related to the fact management warned. The relative attention paid to

 each element may differ when analysts answer retrospective questions and

 when they make their forecasts. Like our subjects in the Simultaneous Warn-

 ing condition, analysts faced with retrospective questions about the effects

 of warning need not evaluate the negative implications of the warning for

 current earnings since they already know the actual earnings-they may

 disregard the temporal nature of the cues. Therefore, the negative im-

 plications of the warning for earnings should receive less attention than

 they would in the actual forecasting environment or our Sequential Warn-

 ing condition, and the positive aspects of warning related to the quality

 of management will be highlighted.

 At the same time, sequential processing of the warning followed by the

 earnings announcement could well lead to more negative reactions. To

 the degree that sequential presentation of the warning and the earnings

 announcement results in less consideration of redundancies between the

 two signals, we would expect a more negative response to firms that warn

 in the actual forecasting environment than in response to retrospective

 questions. Sequential presentation of the warning followed by the actual

 earnings announcement in the actual forecasting environment, and our

 Sequential Warning condition, may also provide analysts with a lower an-

 chor if their initial revised forecast is lower than the previous consensus

 forecast. We cannot directly test in our experiment whether these or other

 particular mechanisms are at work. However, these possibilities, among

 others, suggest that the sequential processing of a warning and an an-

 nouncement is likely to result in lower estimates of future earnings.

 2.4 HYPOTHESES

 In summary, warnings of negative earnings surprises create two coun-

 teracting forces: (a) the effects of analysts' beliefs about differences be-

 tween firms that do and do not warn and (b) the effects of sequentially

 processing the warning and earnings announcement, both of which con-

 tain bad news. Comparing the Simultaneous Warning and No Warning con-

 ditions allows us to test the effects of the warning without the effects of

 sequential processing. Anecdotal evidence predicts analysts will reward

 the warning firms and provide higher future-period earnings estimates

 in the Simultaneous Warning condition than in the No Warning condition.

 Comparing the Sequential Warning and Simultaneous Warning condi-

 tions allows us to assess the effects of adding sequential processing of the

 warning and earnings announcement; since the contents of the warning

 and earnings announcement are the same, the only difference between

 the two conditions is the two-stage sequential revision process allowed

 in the Sequential Warning condition. Based on our discussion above, we

 predict that analysts in the Sequential Warning condition will interpret the

 news as more negative, and they will therefore provide lower estimates of

 future earnings than those in the Simultaneous Warning condition. The

 Simultaneous Warning condition presents subjects with an environment

 similar to that faced by analysts when they answer retrospective ques-

 tions. The Sequential condition presents an environment similar to that

 faced by analysts when revising their forecasts. As a consequence, this

 comparison can be seen as a demonstration of the apparent disconnect

 between analysts' stated beliefs and their actual forecast revisions, since

 all attributes of the firms and their announcements are held constant.

 Finally, evidence from the empirical-archival literature indicating lower

 forecasts for firms that warn of lower earnings (Kasznik and Lev [1995])

 suggests that the cognitive effects of sequentially processing two pieces

 of bad news (the warning and the earnings announcement) overwhelm

 the effects of analysts' beliefs about firms that warn. This implies that the

 estimates of future earnings by the Sequential Warning group will be lower

 than those made by the No Warning group.

 The discussion above suggests the following hypotheses:

 HI: Analysts in the Simultaneous Warning condition will make higher

 estimates of future periods' earnings than those in the No Warning

 condition.

 H2: Analysts in the Sequential Warning condition will make lower esti-

 mates of future periods' earnings than those in the Simultaneous

 Warning condition.

 H3: Analysts in the Sequential Warning condition will make lower esti-

 mates of future periods' earnings than those in the No Warning

 condition.

 3. Method

 Twenty-eight financial analysts (53% buy-side and 47% sell-side; mean

 experience = 2.8 years, standard deviation = 2.08 years, range = 1 to 8

 years)4 from the Singapore offices of six major brokerage and investment

 houses participated in this study. These analysts cover the U.S. and inter-

 national markets, and they had experience making earnings forecasts.

 One of the authors visited each of the firms to administer the research

 instruments. Senior officers from the participating firms were present

 during the administration of the experiment to introduce the author

 and to emphasize the importance of the project.

 The analysts were randomly assigned to one of three between-subjects

 experimental conditions: No Warning, Sequential Warning, and Simultaneous

 Warning. All subjects were first given the background and financial infor-

 mation of the same company in the semiconductor industry, listed on

 4.2 ADDITIONAL ANALYSES

 In this section, we provide preliminary tests of some mechanisms that

 may account for our results. Our primary assertion is that the act of

 sequentially processing the warning and the subsequent earnings an-

 nouncement accounts for the difference between analysts' forecast revi-

 sions and stated beliefs about firms that warn.

 4.2. 1. Warning and Earnings Announcement as Two Pieces of Bad News.

 An implication of our sequential processing argument is that the receipt

 of two pieces of bad news (as in the Sequential Warning condition) could

 lead to more negative reactions. To assess this argument, we first deter-

 mined whether both the warning and the earnings announcement were

 treated as bad news in this condition. Mean initial estimates made in the

 Sequential Warning condition after the warning, but before the earnings

 in response to the warning served as an anchor, analysts who had made

 smaller downward adjustments in response to the warning should have

 anchored on their higher stage-one estimates and produced higher final

 earnings estimates after receipt of the earnings announcement. This im-

 plies a positive correlation between the initial earnings estimates (upon

 receipt of the warning) and final earnings estimates (upon receipt of the

 earnings announcement). Alternatively, analysts in the Sequential Warning

 condition who had inadequately adjusted their forecasts downward upon

 receipt of the warning may have viewed management as not being forth-

 right in their warning when they subsequently received the lower than

 expected actual earnings announcement. One implication of this mech-

 anism is that analysts who had made higher earnings estimates after re-

 ceipt of the warning should make lower earnings estimates upon receipt

 of the earnings announcement to penalize management; that is, the cor-

 relation between the initial and the final earnings estimates should be

 5. Discussion

 In our experiment, analysts in the Simultaneous Warning condition

 made significantly higher earnings estimates than those in the No Warning

 condition, who in turn made higher estimates than those in the Sequential

 Warning condition. These results suggest that any positive effect of ana-

 lysts' beliefs about firms that warn (reflected in their responses to report-

 ers' and our debriefing questions, and in the higher earnings estimates

 provided in the Simultaneous Warning condition relative to the No Warning

 condition) is eliminated by the effect of sequentially processing a warning

 followed by an actual announcement.

 our results is slightly different; at least holding all other things constant

 as we do in our experiment, analysts believe that earnings declines are less

 permanent for firms that warn, but their cognitive processes amplify the

 negative effect of the earnings surprise, and in their forecasts they act as

 though they believe the decline will be more permanent. This apparent

 disconnect between the analysts' attitudes and behavior is actually a com-

 mon finding in the judgment and decision making (see Libby [1981])

 and social psychology literatures (see, e.g., Ajzen and Fishbein [1977] and

 Posavac, Sanbonmatsu, and Fazio [1997]).

 It is our belief that the simultaneous presentation of the warning and

 the earnings announcement eliminates the need to evaluate the implica-

 tions of the warning and thus makes the decision makers more aware

 of management's choice to warn. More simply, they evaluate the Simulta-

 neous Warning condition as presenting a negative cue (lower earnings)

 with a positive one (at least management warned and thus has more in-

 tegrity). Alternatively, when evaluated in sequence, the decision makers

 first face the warning, which is seen as bad news since it is the first indi-

 cation of a significant earnings shortfall. The earnings announcement

 that follows is likely seen simply as confirmation of the prior bad news

 or, in our case, additional bad news. We suspect that our analysts in the

 real-world-like Sequential Warning condition gave short shrift to the pos-

 itive aspects of warning.

 Several limitations of the study should be noted. First, the analysts

 used in our experiment were, on average, less experienced than those
 used in some related studies.18 Consistent with Hunton and McEwen's

 [1997] conclusion that experience is unrelated to forecast accuracy, we

 found no experience effects (see n. 4). However, it is possible that more

 powerful tests would uncover some differences, or that experience dif-

 ferences will be apparent for more complex tasks such as the firm valu-

 ations examined by Hirst and Hopkins [1998]. On a related point, our

 subjects included both buy-side and sell-side analysts, and differences

 may exist between these types of analysts in terms of their forecast accu-

 racy (see Willis [1998]). While we had no reason to expect (and found

 no) systematic differences in their responses to our manipulations, their

 different institutional settings may lead them to make different re-

 sponses in other tasks. Finally, our small sample size limits the power of

 our tests.

 Our results suggest a number of avenues for future research. On the

 archival-empirical side, Soffer, Thiagarajan, and Walther [forthcoming]

 confirm our findings of more negative responses when both the warning

 and actual earnings announcement convey bad news. However, no study

 has yet examined whether the stronger price reaction for warning firms

 is due to actual differences in the persistence of the earnings decline.

 On the experimental side, our results still leave a number of related ques-

 tions unanswered. For example, in our study, initial forecasts after the

 warning were still too high. Other paths may have different effects on

 final postannouncement forecasts (see Soffer, Thiagarajan, and Walther

 [forthcoming] and Matsumoto [1998]). Also, our study focused on warn-

 ings of negative earnings surprises; future research can investigate whether

 the effects we document extend to positive surprises. Our debriefing re-

 sults do suggest that failure to warn can impair the perceived credibility

 of management (Hirst, Koonce, and Miller [1999]), which could affect the

 perceived reliability of other management disclosures (Maines, McDaniel,

 and Harris [1997]). Other potential benefits of warning are also the sub-

 ject for additional research.
