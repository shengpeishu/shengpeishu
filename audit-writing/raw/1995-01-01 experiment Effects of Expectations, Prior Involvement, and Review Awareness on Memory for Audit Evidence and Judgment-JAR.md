1.  Introduction
In this paper, I investigate how the audit decision  process is affected
by prior expectations,  prior audit involvement,  and the review process.
The first two features are inherent in repeat audit engagements,  in which
individuals tend to be assigned repeatedly  to these engagements.  I ex-
amine whether  repeat  engagements  increase  the likelihood  that audi-
tors' judgments  will remain consistent  with prior findings and whether
staff rotations  tend to reduce  any such tendency.  I also assess whether
another control procedure employed in auditing, the review process, can
overcome  consistency  effects.  However,  unlike  prior studies  of  review
processes  (e.g., Trotman [1985] and Libby and Trotman [1992],  but see
Kennedy [1993] for an exception),  I focus on the preventive effect of the
threat of review on the reviewee's decision  process. These attributes in-
teract so that staff rotation and the prospect of review should reduce con-
sistency effects arising from prior involvement  (see, for example, Gibbins
| and Emby [1984],  | Ashton et al. [1989],  |     | and Libby [1989]).  |     |     |

According to AU section 339.02 (AICPA [1988]), working papers doc-
ument work done in the course of the audit and assist in the conduct and
supervision of the audit engagement. Prior years' working papers are
used to plan the current year's audit (Arens and Loebbecke [1988]) and
to predict audit adjustments (Hylas and Ashton [1982]). Often, prior
years' working papers were prepared by current members of the audit
team (i.e., as repeat audit engagements). The extent of repeat audit
engagements and the reassignment of the prior years' team members
provide a potential for efficiency gains and reduced audit risks due to
greater familiarity with the client's system and environment (AICPA
[1978a]). Despite these benefits, staff rotation has been recommended
to introduce a fresh viewpoint (e.g., AICPA [1978a, p. 109]), and this has
been implemented in the form of mandatory partner rotation (AICPA
[1978b, p. 9]). This suggests there could be adverse effects from re-
peated engagements in the form of a reduced ability to bring skepticism
to the audit. In this paper I compare the behavioral effects in both staff
rotation and repeated engagements settings to determine whether staff
rotations imply an improved ability to bring a new viewpoint to the audit,
as claimed in various pronouncements.'
Since one of the benefits of rotation is the introduction of a fresh
perspective, I examine two potential perspective-related gains: greater
attention to inconsistent evidence and a reduced tendency of auditors
to remain congruent with prior findings, given changes in the audit en-
vironment. Differential attention to consistent facts has also been shown
to lead to hindsight bias (Fischhoff and Beyth [1975]) and to over-
confidence (Koriat, Lichtenstein, and Fischhoff [1980]).
An alternative control device is the audit review. Researchers (e.g.,
Solomon [1987]) and professional bodies (AICPA [1988, sec. 230.02])
have attested to the importance of reviews as a quality control procedure.
Generally, such reviews are performed by the audit team. In addition,
the SEC Practice Section requires concurrent partner reviews, as well as
peer reviews at least once every three years (AICPA [1978b, p. 33]). Past
research has emphasized the role of the reviewer in the review process
(Trotman [1985] and Libby and Trotman [1991]). However, consistent
with the literature in social psychology on accountability2 (e.g., see Tet-
lock [1983; 1985; 1987]), I investigate whether the awareness of a review
causes the reviewee to be more vigilant in information processing.
I conducted two experiments to examine these different effects. Using
recall as a proxy for attention (Lynch and Srull [1982]), results from

From an accounting perspective, these findings demonstrate that staff
rotation and review awareness can improve the quality of the audit
decision process.3 The study also adds to the psychological literature
because it provides insights into the cognitive effects of review awareness.
It extends past findings (e.g., Tetlock [1983]) that document increased
cognitive effort (as proxied by recall) with accountability, because it dem-

The experiments were conducted in the context of a financial via-

the audit report.

2.1 EXPECTATION
2.1.1. RecalL While there is some research on the effects of prior
years' working papers on audit judgment (e.g., Wright [1988]), few stud-
ies have examined their effects on memory. Hogarth [1987, pp. 134-35]
argues that memory is reconstructive and is affected by expectations.
Justification for investigating memory effects is twofold. First, memory for
evidence is a key input to the audit decision process. The large volume of
facts auditors are exposed to in the course of an audit, and the length of

be confident in their memories for audit evidence, even when these were
inaccurate (Moeckel and Plumlee [1989]). The implication is that this

other evidence that could affect the audit decision.
I employ the associative network model of impression formation by
Srull and Wyer [1989] as the basis for developing a memory hypothesis.4
In this model, associative links are established among facts during the
encoding process and with a superordinate expectation node. All con-
sistent, inconsistent, and irrelevant facts are organized around the prior
expectation, with consistent facts having stronger direct associations
than inconsistent or irrelevant facts. However, there are no direct links
between any two consistent facts. On the other hand, associations (albeit
weaker) are made between the prior expectation and inconsistent facts,
through the inconsistency resolution process in which attempts are
made to reconcile inconsistent facts with the prior expectation and all
other facts, thereby establishing associative links. A third but weaker
process in Srull and Wyer's [1989] model is bolstering, whereby features
consistent with the expectation are reviewed to confirm their validity.
The relative numbers of consistent and inconsistent facts recalled would
be a function of the strength of the inconsistency resolution activity
compared to the bolstering activity. According to Srull and Wyer [1989],
the inconsistency resolution process generally has priority over the bol-
stering process during information processing, which implies a recall
advantage of inconsistent facts over consistent facts.
In the task used in this study, the conclusions recorded in prior years'
working papers establish expectations about the client's financial via-
bility. The model above predicts that, in general, current-year audit
evidence which is inconsistent with these expectations will be better re-
called than consistent evidence. It should be noted that positive and
negative facts are consistent and inconsistent respectively with a positive
expectation (expectation of financial viability) but vice versa with a
negative expectation (expectation of failure). This suggests that the
number of positive facts recalled minus the number of negative facts re-
called (net-recall) will be smaller with positive expectations than with
negative expectations.

2.1.2. Judgment. Given prior expectations, the psychological litera-
ture has found both contrast and assimilation effects on subsequent
judgments. Assimilation effects are manifested when evaluations are
consistent with an expectation, whereas contrast effects occur when
judgments are made in a direction away from the expectation (Sherif,
Taub, and Hovland [1961]). With prior expectations, an assimilation
effect has been found to dominate with moderate discrepancies and am-
biguous stimuli (Herr, Sherman, and Fazio [1983]).
Assessment of a firm's financial viability is a complex and ambiguous
task. In an auditing context, discrepancies between prior expectations
and the current findings for a particular firm are generally moderate,
because conclusions in previous years are usually 5good predictors of
current findings (Kreutzfeldt and Wallace [1986]). Thus, an assimila-
tion effect is predicted in that auditors are expected to make judgments
consistent with expectations formed using prior years' audit conclu-
sions. The hypothesis is expressed below:
H2: Auditors with positive expectations will make current-year judg-
ments that are more positive than those made by auditors with nega-
tive expectations.
2.2. PRIOR INVOLVEMENT
Although involvement with prior audits can vary by degree, I consider
just two cases: prior involvement and no prior involvement. Auditors
with prior audit involvement are those who generated audit conclusions
previously. Auditors without prior involvement are those with no first-
hand contact with prior audits but with access to prior years' working
papers. To the extent that relevant information has been recorded in
the workpapers, these auditors will have the same knowledge about the
written audit findings and procedures as those with prior involvement in
the audit. The key difference is that the prior years' evaluations of au-
ditors with prior involvement were self-generated, while those without
prior involvement were inherited.

2.2.2. Judgment. The degree of involvement has been found to be in-
versely related to attitude change (Sherif et al. [ 1973] ). Making an initial
judgment has been found to lead to escalating commitment in that fu-
ture decisions are biased toward confirming the decision or committing
more resources to it (Staw [1976]). Four contributors to commitment
have been identified by Salancik [1977], namely, explicitness, publicity,
irrevocability, and volition. These elements are present in an audit set-
ting, since, for example, the material in working papers is generally ex-
plicit, public, irrevocable, and made volitionally. As a result, an auditor
on a repeat engagement may become more committed to the judgments
documented in the prior years' working papers relative to another au-
ditor without prior involvement. Not all four contributors are necessary
to induce commitment; volition alone can increase commitment (Kiesler
[1971, p. 33]). Following Staw [1976], escalation is expected when a de-
cision maker personally makes a decision and then encounters new and
contradictory information.
Commitment may result from a desire to preserve self-esteem and to
meet institutional emphases on consistency (Staw [1981]). An economic
explanation for the escalation effect is that a change in a prior decision
reveals private information about the decision maker's lack of good
judgment (Kanodia, Bushman, and Dickhaut [1989]). Church [1991]
found that auditors committed to a hypothesis about the source of an
error allocated more time to investigate that source. Similarly, Plumlee
[1985] demonstrated that internal auditors who reviewed a system they
designed were more likely to underestimate the seriousness of malfunc-
tions in the system.
In the auditing context, changes in opinions over time may be seen to
reflect inadequate past performance. Since these opinions are clearly
documented and easily accessible in the working papers, any opinion
change is likely to be noticed and require justification. Auditors who are
confronted with new information contradicting their previous decisions

2.3. AWARENESS OF REVIEW
A key feature of the audit environment is the use of reviews as a con-
trol device (Trotman [1985] and Libby and Trotman [1991]). While
existing research has emphasized the role of the reviewer, it is conceiv-
able that because audit reviews are interactive, mere awareness of a po-
tential review could affect the audit process. That is, awareness that a
review is possible may raise the stakes involved since the review process
is an input to the performance evaluation process. Curley, Yates, and
Abrams [1986] argued that a decision maker forms decisions perceived
to be most justifiable to others who may evaluate the decisions. What is
justifiable in a particular situation is not clear, and two possible effects
may arise from the awareness of a potential review.
First, if the reviewee thinks the reviewer could notice evidence in-
congruent with the reported decision, the result could be increased
vigilance and effort. Tetlock [1983; 1987] showed that subjects held
accountable for their decisions were motivated to consider both sides of
an issue in order to hedge against possible criticisms. Kennedy [1993]
found that M.B.A. students made accountable expended more effort
and were not susceptible to the recency bias. Finally, accountability
was found to induce greater consensus and self-insight among auditors
(Johnson and Kaplan [1992]) and to reduce their likelihood of issuing
unqualified opinions (Lord [1992]). These studies suggest that auditors
warned of a potential review of their decisions by a reviewer with access
to the supporting evidence will be more vigilant and pay greater atten-
tion to evidence that is inconsistent with the prior year's judgment. Cur-
rent year's judgments made by the reviewee will also be more moderate,
given that extreme judgments generally require stronger justification.
Second, auditors aware of a review may wish to present an image of
consistency to the reviewer and to establish legitimate grounds for past
decisions (Salancik [1977]). Apprehension of an unfavorable evaluation
has been found to increase commitment (Fox and Staw [1979]). Fol-
lowing these arguments, auditors with prior involvement who are aware
that the reviewer may examine their past judgments will be motivated to
appear consistent. If this is the case, auditors will tend to pay more at-
tention to consistent evidence than inconsistent evidence and to make
judgments that are more consistent with the prior years' judgments.
This discussion implies that review awareness may increase or decrease
the tendency to be consistent with prior outcomes. One way to distinguish
the conflicting effects of review awareness is to differentiate between
accountability for outcome and accountability for process (Simonson and
Staw [1992]). In studies where review awareness increased the tendency
toward consistency, subjects were made responsible for outcomes of their

3. Experiment 1
3.1 OVERVIEW
Subjects were randomly assigned to one of four treatment conditions
resulting from the manipulated between-subjects factors, Expectation
(Positive, Negative) and Involvement (Self-Generated, Inherited). They
performed a two-stage financial viability prediction task. In stage 1,
subjects read 13 facts about the 1988 audit. The first three facts con-
tained background information on the firm and were given to all sub-
jects. The other ten facts indicated the financial viability of the firm
and were largely positive for subjects in the Positive Expectation condi-
tion and largely negative for subjects in the Negative Expectation con-
dition. After reading the facts, subjects in the Self-Generated condition
made an evaluation of the firm's financial viability, while those in the
Inherited condition read the evaluation made by the previous auditor.
In stage 2, subjects read a separate list of 30 facts pertaining to the
firm's condition in 19896 and then made a financial viability assess-
ment. Thereafter, a distraction task was given, followed by free recall of
the stage 2 facts. A debriefing questionnaire was administered at the
end of the session.

3.2 SUBJECTS
Subjects participating in Experiment 1 were employed by six mid-
western regional offices of a Big Six public accounting firm. The author
made personal visits to each office to administer the experimental ses-
sions. Twelve auditors were involved in the pretests and the remaining
92 auditors took part in the main experiment. The subjects in Experi-
ment 1 were 52 seniors with a mean experience of 3.7 years and 24
managers with a mean experience of 8.6 years.7 There is evidence that
auditors with about three years of experience are familiar with aspects
of a going-concern evaluation such as assessments of a client's business,
key operating factors, and profitability (Abdolmohammadi [1990]).
Senior audit partners at the regional offices confirmed that managers
and seniors with at least three years of audit experience were involved
in the various stages of assessing a firm's financial viability.
3.3 INDEPENDENT VARIABLES
The design was a 2 x 2 between-subjects ANOVA,w ith the factors of in-
terest being Involvement and Expectation. The factor Expectation had
two levels-Positive Expectation (expectation of viability) and Negative
Expectation (expectation of failure). Expectation was manipulated by
varying the facts subjects received in stage 1. Involvement was a between-
subjects factor with two levels: Self-Generated and Inherited. In the Self-
Generated condition, subjects made judgments in stages 1 and 2. They
were also instructed to be prepared to justify their initial judgments,
because this manipulation has been found to be effective in inducing
subjects to demonstrate consistency in their judgments (Hagafors and
Brehmer [1983] and Ashton [1990]). Subjects in the Inherited condi-
tion did not personally make the prior year's judgment, nor were they
told to be prepared to justify their decisions. Instead, they read the stage
1 judgment assigned to them and then made a judgment in stage 2.
3.4 DEPENDENT VARIABLES
Two dependent variables were measured: memory for the different
types of audit evidence and judgment of the firm's current financial via-
bility. Memory was measured by counting the number of positive and
negative stage 2 facts recalled. The financial viability assessments in stages
1 and 2 were made on 15-point bipolar scales, with the two extreme an-
chors being -7 ("definitely fail") and +7 ("definitely viable") respectively.8

3.5 STIMULUS MATERIALS
The ten stage 1 facts were selected to portray either a predominantly
positive or negative expectation of the firm's financial viability, using
cues on the firm's profitability, liquidity, and financial support from
creditors and owners. Stage 2 facts were selected from a set of 41 state-
ments developed by reference to prior research (Kida [1984] and Choo
and Trotman [1991]), audit manuals of a Big Six public accounting
firm, and ValueL ine reports. These statements were pretested by seven
audit seniors (mean experience 3.1 years) and five audit managers
(mean experience 6.3 years) from a Big Six firm. Subjects rated the 41
facts on their implications for the firm's financial viability, using a 15-
point bipolar scale. The mean correlation of each subject's rating with
the other subjects was 0.64. Based on these ratings, I selected two sets of
ten facts each, one positive and one negative, such that their mean ab-
solute ratings across subjects were not significantly different (p = 0.999).
Positive facts had a mean rating of 2.76 and negative facts a mean rating
of -2.73. Ten irrelevant (i.e., neutral) facts were also selected, with a
mean rating of 0.29.9
Thus, the final stage 2 list which subjects read consisted of 30 state-
ments: 10 indicating financial distress, 10 indicating financial viability,
and 10 irrelevant to financial viability. Of the ten irrelevant facts, three
were positioned at the start and three at the end of the passage to com-
pensate for primacy and recency effects in memory (Crowder [1976]).
The other four irrelevant facts were distributed throughout the rest of
the list to serve as distractors and make the task less transparent. Other
than the restriction on placement of irrelevant facts, order of the other
facts was random across subjects.

4.5 DISCUSSION
The results of Experiment 1 suggest that rotation results in better at-
tention to current-year facts that are inconsistent with the prior year's
evaluation. Since differential attention to evidence affects what gets re-
corded in the working papers, as well as the search for further evidence,
this finding has important audit implications. In contrast, the impact of
rotation on the audit judgment is less clear, although there is some evi-
dence that the new auditor is less disposed to make current judgments
consistent with prior judgments.

6. Conclusion
This paper reports the results of two experiments to investigate the im-
pact of two institutional features of auditing: staff rotation and audit re-
view. Although cognitive factors are also important, the results obtained
can be viewed in terms of intrinsic incentives (Libby and Lipe [1992])
or pressures (Ashton [1990]) that auditors face. The results of Experi-
ment 1 suggest that prior audit involvement entails social pressures to re-
main consistent. Likewise, review awareness, examined in Experiment 2,
induces a form of social pressure which reduces consistency effects.

lack of realism of the audit task and setting. Still, in actual organizational
settings, premiums are placed on the enhancement of self-image and or-
